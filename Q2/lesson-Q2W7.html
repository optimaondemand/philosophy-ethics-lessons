📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Applied ethics is not theory made simpler. It is theory made harder.
This lesson examines three domains where consequentialism, deontology, and emerging frameworks are tested against real decisions: medical ethics, the ethics of war, and the ethics of technology (including artificial intelligence). Each domain has generated systematic applied ethics frameworks. Understanding how and why they take the shape they do requires the theoretical foundations from the previous five lessons.

📚 PART 1 · MEDICAL ETHICS: THE BELMONT PRINCIPLES
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
A doctor believes a particular treatment is best for a patient and wants to proceed. The patient, fully informed, declines. What should the doctor do?

The Belmont Report (1979)

Medical ethics as a formal discipline emerged partly in response to historical abuses: most notably the Tuskegee Syphilis Study (1932–1972), in which researchers withheld known effective treatment from Black men with syphilis without their knowledge or consent, in order to study the disease's progression. The National Commission for the Protection of Human Subjects produced the Belmont Report in 1979, which established the framework that governs research ethics and, by extension, much of clinical ethics today.

Principle What it requires Theoretical root
Respect for Persons (Autonomy) Treat individuals as autonomous agents; protect those with diminished autonomy. Kantian: respect for rational agency
Beneficence Maximize benefits; minimize harm to research subjects and patients. Consequentialist: maximize well-being
Non-maleficence "First, do no harm." Some actions are prohibited regardless of potential benefit. Deontological: some actions are inherently prohibited
Justice Distribute benefits and burdens fairly; do not exploit vulnerable populations. Rawlsian/Kantian: fairness and equal dignity

Notice that the four principles draw from both consequentialist and deontological traditions. Medical ethics did not choose one theory and apply it; it drew on both because real medical situations call on both. This is characteristic of applied ethics: the question is not which theory is correct in the abstract, but which framework's insight is most relevant to the specific ethical dimension of the case at hand.

📚 PART 2 · THE ETHICS OF WAR: JUST WAR THEORY

A Tradition With Ancient Roots

Just war theory is the most developed moral framework for evaluating the use of military force. It has roots in Cicero, developed fully in Augustine (354–430) and Aquinas (1225–1274), and has been refined through modern international law. The framework divides into two parts: jus ad bellum (the justice of going to war) and jus in bello (justice in the conduct of war).

Jus ad bellum: When is war justified?
  • Just cause (defense, not aggression)
  • Right intention
  • Legitimate authority
  • Last resort
  • Reasonable chance of success
  • Proportionality of ends
Jus in bello: How must war be conducted?
  • Discrimination (combatants vs. noncombatants)
  • Proportionality of means
  • No use of inherently evil means (e.g., torture of prisoners)
  • Treatment of prisoners and wounded

Crucially, jus in bello requirements apply independently of jus ad bellum: a war that is just in its cause can still be conducted unjustly, and a soldier fighting in an unjust war is still bound by the in bello constraints. This independence reflects the deontological insight that some actions are prohibited regardless of the justice of the overall cause. The discrimination principle — the requirement not to deliberately target noncombatants — is central to evaluating the Hiroshima decision from Lesson 2.01.

📚 PART 3 · TECHNOLOGY AND ARTIFICIAL INTELLIGENCE

New Questions, Old Framework

Artificial intelligence raises ethical questions that are genuinely new in their specific form but philosophically familiar in their structure. The 2017 Asilomar AI Principles — developed by a consortium of AI researchers — identified several core ethical concerns: safety (the AI should not harm users or others), fairness (the AI should not discriminate unjustly across groups), transparency (users should be able to understand how the AI makes decisions), privacy (the AI should not violate users' informational autonomy), and accountability (someone should be responsible for the AI's actions).

Each of these maps onto the theoretical frameworks you have learned. Safety and fairness are partly consequentialist concerns: an AI that produces harmful or discriminatory outcomes is doing moral damage that counts against it. Transparency and privacy are deontological: they protect the rational autonomy of persons from being manipulated or used as data resources without consent. Accountability is a question about moral responsibility: when an AI system makes a harmful decision, who is the moral agent responsible?

The question of moral agency is particularly interesting from the standpoint of the course. Kant defined moral agents as rational beings capable of acting from duty. AI systems, however sophisticated, do not act from duty: they optimize for specified objectives, which is closer to the consequentialist picture. But they cannot reflect on whether those objectives are the right ones — that remains a function of the humans who design and deploy them.

💪 WHAT YOU HAVE LEARNED

Applied ethics is not simpler than theoretical ethics: it is theory tested under the pressure of real decisions, real stakes, and real conflicts between competing principles. Medical ethics, just war theory, and AI ethics all show that consequentialist and deontological insights are not competitors but complementary lenses. Neither, by itself, gives a complete picture. The next lesson examines other modern moral frameworks — intuitionism and social contract theory — that address what consequentialism and deontology each leave out.
