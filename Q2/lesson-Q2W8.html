📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
What if moral knowledge does not always come through theory?
Both consequentialism and deontology are systematic theories: they offer a single principle from which all moral judgments are derived. But some of the most influential modern moral philosophers have argued that systematic theory misrepresents how moral knowledge actually works. This lesson examines three alternatives: moral intuitionism, which holds that some moral truths are directly known; social contract theory, which grounds morality in rational agreement; and moral particularism, which holds that there are no universal moral principles at all.

Ross and Prima Facie Duties

W.D. Ross argued in The Right and the Good (1930) that moral knowledge does not come primarily from theory: it comes from moral intuitions that any sufficiently reflective person can recognize. We know, for example, that gratitude is owed to benefactors, that we should keep our promises, and that we should not harm innocent people. These are not deductions from a first principle; they are direct moral perceptions.

Ross called these prima facie duties: duties that genuinely bind us, but which can be overridden when a stronger duty applies. His list included: fidelity (keeping promises), reparation (making amends for wrongs done), gratitude, justice, beneficence (improving others' condition), self-improvement, and non-maleficence. When two prima facie duties conflict, we must exercise moral judgment to determine which is stronger in the circumstances — there is no algorithm for this, only practical wisdom.

Ross is important because he preserves what is compelling about both consequentialism and deontology (beneficence and non-maleficence, fidelity and justice) without reducing morality to a single principle. But his framework raises a question: if there is no principle for adjudicating between prima facie duties, what prevents moral judgment from collapsing into mere personal preference? Ross's answer is that moral perception is like perceptual knowledge of the external world: reliable in most cases, correctable through reflection and conversation, but not reducible to a formula.

📚 PART 2 · RAWLS AND THE SOCIAL CONTRACT

Justice as Fairness

John Rawls (1921–2002) revived social contract theory in A Theory of Justice (1971), the most influential work of political philosophy published in the 20th century. Social contract theories, originating with Hobbes, Locke, and Rousseau, ground political and moral obligations in a hypothetical agreement among rational persons. Rawls designed a specific thought experiment: the original position behind a veil of ignorance.

Imagine choosing the basic structure of society before you know which position you will occupy in it: your natural abilities, your social class, your race, your generation, your conception of the good. Behind this veil of ignorance, what principles of justice would rational persons choose? Rawls argued they would choose two principles: first, the greatest equal scheme of basic liberties for all; second, the difference principle: social and economic inequalities are permissible only if they benefit the least advantaged members of society.

RAWLS'S ARGUMENT STRUCTURE
Original position: choose principles without knowing your place in society
→
Rational persons choose principles that protect the worst-off position
→
The difference principle: inequalities only justified if they benefit the least advantaged

Rawls's theory is neither purely consequentialist (it does not maximize total welfare) nor purely deontological (it does not derive everything from the categorical imperative). It is a procedural theory: just principles are those that would be agreed to under conditions of fair reasoning. This gives it a different character from both Bentham and Kant, and it has been enormously influential in political philosophy and policy debates about inequality, healthcare, education, and taxation.

📚 PART 3 · MORAL PARTICULARISM

The Rejection of Universal Principles

Jonathan Dancy, in Moral Reasons (1993), advanced a position that challenges every theory we have examined: moral particularism. The claim is that there are no universal moral principles. Any feature of a situation that counts as a moral reason in favor of an action in one case may count against the action in another case, or be morally neutral in a third. What makes lying wrong in one case is not transferable as a principle to all other cases; you must look at the whole moral texture of each particular situation.

Particularism is a serious challenge, but it faces a serious objection: if there are no generalizable moral principles, moral reasoning becomes impossible to share or evaluate. We need principles precisely because we want to be able to explain our moral judgments to each other, hold each other accountable, and build institutions that treat like cases alike. A morality that cannot be stated in any general form collapses into a sophisticated form of the intuitions-all-the-way-down view that Ross was trying to avoid.

The deepest problem with particularism: even its advocates make implicit generalizations. When Dancy says "look carefully at the whole texture of the particular situation," he is offering a general methodological principle. When he says "no feature automatically counts as a reason everywhere," he is making a universal claim about moral reasons. It appears impossible to do moral philosophy without some level of generalization.

What particularism does usefully show is the danger of mechanical application of principles without attention to particular circumstances. Aristotle would agree with this much: moral wisdom (phronesis) is precisely the capacity to recognize what is called for in a particular situation, not a matter of applying an algorithm. This is one reason Quarter 3's virtue ethics is not fully captured by any modern principle-based theory.

💪 WHAT YOU HAVE LEARNED

Intuitionism, social contract theory, and particularism each respond to something the major systematic theories get wrong or leave out. Ross preserves the multiplicity of moral obligations without reducing them to one principle. Rawls develops a powerful procedural account of justice. Dancy challenges the very assumption that universalizable principles can capture moral truth.
