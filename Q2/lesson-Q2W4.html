Act and Rule: Applying and Testing Utilitarianism

📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
When you apply the utilitarian calculus to specific cases, do you calculate the expected utility of each individual act, or of the rule that prescribes it?
The distinction between act-utilitarianism and rule-utilitarianism is not a minor technical refinement. It reveals a fundamental tension in consequentialism between two things most people care about: producing the best outcomes in specific situations, and following rules that, if generally adhered to, produce the best outcomes overall. The difference between these two can, in difficult cases, be a matter of life and death.

📚 PART 1 · ACT VS. RULE UTILITARIANISM
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
You are driving and could save several minutes by running a red light when no cars are visible. Should you run it?

Act-Utilitarianism

Act-utilitarianism holds that the right action in any given situation is the one that produces the greatest expected utility for that particular situation. You calculate the expected pleasures and pains of each available option for all affected parties, and choose the option with the highest net sum. The calculation is done act by act.

This version is maximally flexible: it does not restrict you to following moral rules when a specific situation calls for breaking them. But it faces serious objections. First, it requires a calculation that is rarely possible in practice: we do not know the full consequences of our actions. Second, it seems to permit — or even require — terrible acts when the arithmetic favors them. If framing an innocent person for a crime would prevent a riot that would harm many others, the act-utilitarian appears to be committed to framing the innocent person.

Rule-Utilitarianism

Rule-utilitarianism holds that the right action is the one prescribed by a set of moral rules that, if generally followed, would produce the greatest overall utility. You do not calculate the utility of each act; you follow the rule whose general observance produces the best consequences. Rules like "keep your promises" and "do not frame innocent people" pass this test because a society in which these rules are generally followed is better off than one in which they are not.

Rule-utilitarianism captures more of our moral intuitions because it preserves stable rules. But critics argue it is either redundant or unstable: if a rule produces better consequences when generally followed, then following it in a specific case where it would not produce better consequences seems like a failure to be a proper consequentialist. Rule-utilitarianism may smuggle in rule-following commitments that are really deontological, not consequentialist.

Act-Utilitarianism
Evaluate each individual act by its expected utility in the specific situation.
Strength: flexible. Weakness: can license terrible acts in unusual situations.
Rule-Utilitarianism
Follow the rule that, if generally observed, produces the greatest utility.
Strength: protects individuals. Weakness: may not be a pure form of consequentialism.
📚 PART 2 · THE JUSTICE OBJECTION

Scapegoating and the Tyranny of the Majority

The most persistent objection to utilitarianism, raised by John Rawls and many others, concerns justice. If the right action is the one that maximizes total welfare, what happens when the greatest welfare for the majority requires sacrificing the welfare — or the rights — of a minority? Slavery in the antebellum American South might have produced, for a brief historical period, a net positive welfare calculation for the majority of those in society who benefited from it economically. That conclusion is obviously monstrous, but the utilitarian calculus seems to permit it.

Utilitarians respond in several ways. Mill himself was a passionate opponent of slavery and argued that when indirect effects (the corruption of character in slaveholders, the instability produced by injustice, the long-run social costs) are included, slavery fails even the utilitarian calculus. But this response is contested: it is not clear that the argument against slavery should depend on a calculation of indirect effects. If the only argument against enslaving a person is that the indirect costs are too high, then in a world where those costs were lower, slavery would be permissible — and most people believe the wrongness of slavery is not contingent on any such calculation.

Work out this objection in your notebook, then read the analysis below.

The justice objection forces us to ask: is there a morally relevant difference between a situation where 100 people are each slightly harmed, and a situation where 1 person is catastrophically harmed but 100 people are each slightly benefited, producing the same net welfare score?

The utilitarian says no: what matters is the aggregate, and the aggregate is equal in both cases. But most people have a powerful intuition that the distribution of welfare matters morally, not just the total. Rawls argued that a just society is one that could be chosen by people who did not know which position in it they would occupy. Under such a condition, people would not choose a society that concentrated welfare by sacrificing a minority — because they might be in that minority.

This is one of the central objections that drives interest in both deontological ethics (Kant) and virtue ethics (Aristotle): both offer grounds for ruling out certain treatments of persons as intrinsically wrong, not merely as welfare-suboptimal.

💪 WHAT YOU HAVE LEARNED

The act/rule distinction reveals an internal tension in utilitarianism: the theory that seemed to give a clean single principle turns out to generate two different versions that give different answers in important cases. And the justice objection shows that aggregating welfare across persons — treating the total as what matters — may fail to capture something morally fundamental about how individuals must be treated. Quarter 2 now turns to the tradition that builds its ethics precisely on that idea: the deontological ethics of Immanuel Kant.
