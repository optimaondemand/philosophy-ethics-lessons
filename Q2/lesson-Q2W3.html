The Greatest Happiness Principle: Bentham, Mill, and Classical Utilitarianism

📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
What exactly is the good that morality should produce, and how do we measure it?
Utilitarianism is the most developed, influential, and contested form of consequentialism. Its founders, Jeremy Bentham (1748–1832) and John Stuart Mill (1806–1873), gave the theory a specific answer to the question of what "good consequences" means: happiness, understood as the presence of pleasure and the absence of pain. This lesson examines their answers, the differences between their versions, and the serious philosophical difficulties each faces.

Jeremy Bentham: Pleasure, Pain, and the Calculus

Jeremy Bentham opens his Introduction to the Principles of Morals and Legislation (1789) with one of the most direct sentences in the history of ethics: "Nature has placed mankind under the governance of two sovereign masters, pain and pleasure. It is for them alone to point out what we ought to do, as well as to determine what we shall do." On Bentham's view, pleasure is the only intrinsic good; pain is the only intrinsic evil; and the morally right action is the one that maximizes the net sum of pleasure over pain across all affected parties.

To make this practical, Bentham proposed a hedonic calculus with seven dimensions along which any pleasure or pain can be measured:

For Bentham, a pushpin (a simple game) that produces great pleasure is morally equal to poetry that produces great pleasure — pleasures differ only in quantity, not in kind. "The quantity of pleasure being equal," he wrote, "pushpin is as good as poetry." This was deliberate: Bentham believed moral philosophy should be a science of human welfare, free from elitist judgments about which pleasures are more "elevated" than others.

📚 PART 2 · MILL'S REVISION: HIGHER AND LOWER PLEASURES

John Stuart Mill: Not All Pleasures Are Equal

Mill accepted the utilitarian framework but was uncomfortable with Bentham's purely quantitative calculus. It seems implausible that a life of intense but shallow pleasures — constant entertainment, physical comfort, trivial amusements — is superior to a life of intellectual engagement, love, and creative achievement, simply if the former contains more hedonic units. Mill introduced a qualitative distinction between higher and lower pleasures.

Higher pleasures include intellectual activity, aesthetic appreciation, moral and civic engagement, and friendship. Lower pleasures are primarily physical and sensory. Mill's test for which pleasures are higher: those who have experienced both, and are in a position to compare them, almost universally prefer the higher pleasures even when they involve more effort and less immediate satisfaction. "It is better to be Socrates dissatisfied than a fool satisfied," Mill wrote in one of his most famous lines.

BENTHAM
All pleasures are equal in kind
Only differ in intensity, duration, and the other calculus dimensions. A simple game producing great pleasure is worth as much as a great poem producing equal pleasure.
MILL
Pleasures differ in quality as well as quantity
Higher pleasures (intellectual, moral, aesthetic) are superior in kind, not just in quantity. A small amount of higher pleasure may outweigh a large amount of lower pleasure.

Mill's revision helps avoid some of Bentham's counterintuitive results. But it raises a new problem: if some pleasures are qualitatively superior, on what basis? If the answer is that they contribute to a more fully human life or to human flourishing, Mill seems to be quietly importing a non-hedonist account of the good — one closer to Aristotle's — into what is supposed to be a pleasure-based theory.

📚 PART 3 · PROBLEMS WITH HEDONISM

The Experience Machine

The philosopher Robert Nozick offered a famous challenge to hedonism in his Anarchy, State, and Utopia (1974): the experience machine. Imagine a device that can give you any experiences you desire for the rest of your life — the feelings of writing a great novel, making close friends, contributing to the world, achieving difficult things. From the inside, you cannot tell the difference between the machine's simulations and reality. The only cost: you float in a tank, disconnected from any actual engagement with the world.

Most people, upon reflection, say they would not permanently connect. And their reasons reveal something important: people do not only care about having pleasant experiences; they care about actually doing things, actually being certain kinds of people, actually relating to the real world. If hedonism were correct, refusing the machine would make no sense. The fact that most people refuse it suggests that hedonism does not capture what humans actually value.

💪 WHAT YOU HAVE LEARNED

Bentham and Mill gave consequentialism its most precise form by identifying happiness with pleasure and the absence of pain. Bentham tried to make this measurable; Mill recognized that pleasures differ in quality as well as quantity. But Nozick's experience machine reveals that hedonism may rest on an incomplete account of what humans value. The next lesson examines how consequentialism works in practice, and the problems that arise when we try to apply the utilitarian calculus to real cases.
