📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Why are the basic goods not merely desirable but obligatory?
The previous lesson identified the basic human goods: the things toward which human beings are naturally oriented. But there is a gap between "this is a good for me" and "I am obligated to pursue it and not act against it." A person might acknowledge that health is a human good while claiming no obligation to pursue it. Natural law theory must explain the source of moral obligation: why the basic goods are not merely values to be pursued but constraints on action that may not be violated. This lesson examines Aquinas's and Finnis's accounts of obligation and their differences from Kant's account.

📚 PART 1 · THE SOURCE OF OBLIGATION
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
What makes something morally obligatory rather than merely advisable? Is there a difference between "you should do this if you want to flourish" and "you must not do this regardless of what you want"?

Aquinas on the Source of Obligation

For Aquinas, moral obligation is grounded in the participation of human reason in the eternal law governing all creation. Natural law is not merely a description of what human beings happen to desire; it is the rational order to which human beings, as rational beings, are bound by their nature. The basic goods are not merely useful or enjoyable; they are genuine goods, and reason recognizes their genuine goodness as a claim on action. To act against a genuine good is to act against reality as reason perceives it.

The critical distinction is between "this action would harm a good" and "this action directly intends to harm a good." Aquinas permits actions that have harmful side effects (under the doctrine of double effect, which you will study in detail in Lesson 4.06) but prohibits actions that directly target a basic good for destruction. The reason is that directly intending the destruction of a good is to treat something genuinely valuable as if it were not: it is a fundamental misuse of practical reason.

Finnis's Requirements of Practical Reasonableness

Finnis translates this into a set of nine requirements of practical reasonableness: principles that specify how a person who is reasoning well about what to do will structure her choices and actions. The most important for understanding moral obligation are:

FINNIS'S KEY REQUIREMENTS OF PRACTICAL REASONABLENESS
No Arbitrary Preferences
You may not arbitrarily prefer some instances of a basic good over others, or prefer one person's good to another's without relevant justification.
Commitment to the Common Good
You must take seriously the good of others, not just your own. A life plan that simply ignores the good of other persons is not fully reasonable.
Do Not Act Directly Against a Basic Good
The most important requirement: you may not choose an action that directly intends the destruction of a basic good, whether in yourself or in others. This generates the absolute prohibitions of natural law.
Efficiency and Proportionality
Among available options that do not violate the absolute prohibition, choose the one that most effectively promotes the relevant goods at stake. Don't waste what is genuinely good.
📚 PART 2 · ABSOLUTE PROHIBITIONS

Why Some Prohibitions Are Absolute

Natural law theory holds that some moral prohibitions are absolute: they admit of no exceptions, regardless of consequences. These are the prohibitions against directly intending the destruction of a basic good. The reason is not that natural law is dogmatic or indifferent to consequences, but that the structure of the basic goods makes exceptions impossible to justify.

Recall that the basic goods are incommensurable. If you cannot measure the good of one person against the good of another on a common scale, then you cannot justify destroying one person's basic good by pointing to a greater total good. The consequentialist calculation requires precisely this common scale. Natural law theory denies the scale exists; therefore it denies the calculation is valid. The prohibition on deliberately killing an innocent person, for example, is not just a very strong rule: it is the recognition that there is no perspective from which such a killing could be justified by "better outcomes."

Think about this first, then open: Does natural law theory say that bad consequences are irrelevant to moral decisions? Write or think through your answer before opening.
No. Natural law theory does not ignore consequences. Among actions that do not violate the absolute prohibitions, consequences matter significantly: the requirement of efficiency and proportionality says that you should choose the action that best promotes the relevant goods. What natural law denies is that consequences can justify crossing the absolute prohibition against directly acting against a basic good. There is a difference between "consequences determine everything" (consequentialism) and "consequences are relevant within moral limits" (natural law). Natural law accepts the latter and rejects the former.

Natural Law vs. Kant on Obligation

Question Kant Natural Law (Aquinas/Finnis)
Source of obligation Pure practical reason: the categorical imperative The nature of the basic human goods and their claim on reason
Role of human nature Irrelevant: moral law is derived from reason alone, not from human nature or inclinations Essential: what human beings are naturally oriented toward provides the content of natural law
What does obligation feel like from the inside? A constraint imposed by reason on inclination; often experienced against the grain of desire Ideally an expression of one's own nature; experienced as a constraint only when one's desires are disordered
Are there absolute prohibitions? Yes: the categorical imperative generates absolute prohibitions (e.g., never use a person merely as a means) Yes: the prohibition on directly acting against a basic good admits no exceptions

💪 WHAT YOU HAVE LEARNED

Moral obligation in natural law theory is grounded in the nature of the basic human goods and the demands of practical reasonableness. Some prohibitions are absolute: they cannot be overridden by consequences, because the incommensurability of the basic goods means there is no common scale on which such trade-offs can be calculated. Consequences remain relevant within the limits the absolute prohibitions establish. The next lesson examines what natural law theory means by morality itself: the full account of why morality is not merely social convention but a genuine dimension of human reality.
