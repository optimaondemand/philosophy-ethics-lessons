The First Principle of Natural Law: Do Good and Avoid Evil
Lesson
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
What does "the good" mean for natural law theory, and why is "do good and avoid evil" more than a tautology?
The first and most basic precept of natural law, according to Aquinas, is: do good and avoid evil. To a modern ear this sounds either trivially obvious or dangerously vague. But Aquinas means something very precise by it: it is the formal principle governing all practical reasoning, and it presupposes a particular account of what goodness is. This lesson explains what Aquinas means by "good," why the first precept is the foundation of all subsequent natural law precepts, and how this differs from both utilitarian and Kantian accounts of goodness.

I will be able to explain what Aquinas means by "good" and why he defines it as what all things seek (bonum est quod omnia appetunt).
2

3
I will be able to explain how this principle differs from the utilitarian account of the good and the Kantian account of duty.
📚 PART 1 · WHAT IS THE GOOD?
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
When you say something is "good," what do you mean? Is goodness a feature of the thing itself, a relation between the thing and your desire, or something else?

Bonum Est Quod Omnia Appetunt

Aquinas opens Question 94 of the Summa Theologica with a definition drawn from Aristotle: "the good is what all things seek" (bonum est quod omnia appetunt). This is not a definition of goodness as whatever happens to be wanted; it is a claim about the structure of being: all things, by their nature, are oriented toward their own perfection or fulfillment. A plant is oriented toward growth and reproduction; an animal toward sensory flourishing and survival; a human being toward rational activity and flourishing. Goodness, for Aquinas, is being insofar as it is desirable or fulfilling for something of a given nature.

This has an important consequence: evil is not a positive force in opposition to good but an absence, a privation. Evil is what frustrates or falls short of a thing's proper end. Pain, ignorance, and injustice are evil not because some cosmic force made them so, but because they are opposed to the natural orientation of human beings toward health, knowledge, and fair dealing.

AQUINAS, SUMMA THEOLOGICA I-II, QUESTION 94, ARTICLE 2
"The first precept of law is this: Good is to be done and pursued, and evil is to be avoided."
Latin: Bonum est faciendum et prosequendum, et malum vitandum.
📚 PART 2 · THE FIRST PRECEPT AS FOUNDATION

The Foundational Role of the First Precept

Aquinas is careful to distinguish between the first precept as a formal principle of practical reason and the more specific precepts that follow from it. The first precept does not tell you what is good; it tells you the structure of all genuine moral reasoning: it moves toward the good and away from evil. All subsequent natural law precepts derive their obligatory force from this principle: "preserve human life" is a precept because human life is a good; "seek truth" is a precept because truth is a good toward which human reason is naturally ordered.

This is structurally analogous to the role of the principle of non-contradiction in theoretical reason. The principle "A thing cannot both be and not be at the same time and in the same respect" does not tell you anything specific about what is true; it tells you the structure within which true propositions must fall. Similarly, "do good and avoid evil" does not tell you what is good; it tells you the structure of practical reason that all genuine moral norms must instantiate.

Comparing the Three Theories

Question Utilitarianism Kantian Deontology Natural Law (Aquinas)
What is good? Happiness, pleasure, welfare, or preference satisfaction. The only unconditional good is a good will. What fulfills the natural orientation of human beings toward their proper end.
Where does goodness come from? From the subjective states of sentient beings. From conformity to the demands of rational agency. From the structure of being and nature: things are good because of what they are, not because they are desired.
What is the first principle of ethics? Maximize utility. Act only according to the categorical imperative. Do good and avoid evil.
Think about this first, then open: The utilitarian says that goodness is determined by what people actually desire. What is the sharpest natural law objection to this? Write or think through your answer before clicking.
The natural law objection: If goodness is determined by what people actually desire, then there is no basis for criticizing any desire as misdirected or corrupted. A person who desires to dominate others, or who has been conditioned to prefer degradation to flourishing, would be pursuing a "good" simply because they desire it. Natural law theory argues that this makes morality incoherent: we need a standard for evaluating desires themselves, not merely for summing them up. That standard is what human beings are naturally ordered toward: their proper end. A desire that runs against that end is not merely unusual; it is a disordered desire, one that runs contrary to the agent's genuine good.

💪 WHAT YOU HAVE LEARNED

Aquinas defines good as what all things are naturally oriented toward: their own perfection and fulfillment. This is not a subjective account (goodness is not determined by what anyone happens to desire) but an objective one grounded in the teleological structure of nature. The first precept of natural law, "do good and avoid evil," is the formal foundation of all practical reasoning: it specifies the structure that all genuine moral norms must instantiate. All subsequent natural law precepts follow from this principle by specifying what goods human beings are naturally oriented toward. The next lesson examines those goods in detail: the basic human goods that natural reason discerns as the content of natural law.
