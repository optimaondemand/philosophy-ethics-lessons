📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
What has this year's study of ethics revealed about how to think, how to live, and who to become?
You have studied four of the most important ethical theories in the Western tradition. You have seen them argue with each other across centuries. You have tested them against hard cases, examined their objections, and traced the philosophical conversation from Plato's Socrates to Aristotle to Kant to Aquinas to Finnis. This final lesson does not simply review what you have covered. It asks a different question: given everything you have studied, what has the philosophical inquiry itself revealed? The discipline of ethics is not a collection of answers but a training in how to ask the questions that matter most.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to place all four theories in a single map showing their starting questions, their central claims, and their relationships to each other.
2
I will be able to explain what the study of ethics contributes to the project of living well, and why philosophical training matters for a full human life.
3
I will be able to take and defend a reasoned position on which ethical theory is most adequate, with specific philosophical justification.

📚 PART 2 · THE PHILOSOPHICAL LIFE

Ethics as a Way of Living, Not a Subject to Be Passed

Aristotle opens the Nicomachean Ethics with the observation that every art, inquiry, action, and pursuit aims at some good. He closes it with the observation that the good life for human beings is an activity rather than a possession: eudaimonia is not something you have; it is something you do. The same is true of the philosophical life that ethics describes and demands. You do not possess philosophical wisdom by completing a course; you pursue it by continuing, for the rest of your life, to ask the questions this course has taught you to ask.

Plato's Socrates says in the Apology (38a): "The unexamined life is not worth living for a human being." This is not an arrogant dismissal of ordinary life. It is the claim that human beings, as rational animals, are capable of something that mere living does not exhaust: the examined life, the life lived in the light of reason reflecting on what is genuinely good and how to pursue it. Ethics is the systematic form of that examination. What you have learned this year is not a set of answers but a set of tools and a set of questions that will repay examination for the rest of your life.

PLATO, APOLOGY 38A
"The unexamined life is not worth living for a human being."
Socrates, speaking at his trial in Athens, 399 BC

What This Course Has Given You

At the beginning of this course, you encountered Socrates asking: "How should one live?" You have spent a year learning four rigorous answers to that question from the Western tradition's greatest ethical thinkers. You have seen each answer tested against objections, compared with competitors, and applied to real cases. You know that the question is harder than it looks; you also know that it is not unanswerable, that the tradition has resources for making real progress on it, and that your own reasoning about these questions is now equipped with distinctions, arguments, and examples that most people never encounter.

The most important thing this course has tried to give you is not the answers but the questions: the habit of asking, before any important action, what is genuinely at stake; whose interests are affected; what duties or obligations apply; what kind of person this action will make you; and whether it is consistent with what reason and conscience tell you about the good. That habit of question-asking, persistently applied, is what the philosophical tradition calls practical wisdom. It is, as Aristotle says, the master virtue.

PHILOSOPHY HONORS: ETHICS · COURSE CONCLUSION
The philosophical conversation you entered this year is four thousand years old. You are now part of it.
From Socrates asking his fellow citizens to account for their lives, to Aristotle tracing eudaimonia through virtue and friendship, to Kant demanding that persons never be used as mere means, to Aquinas showing how reason participates in the order of creation, the tradition has asked, again and again, the same question that opens Lesson 1.01: how should one live? There is no final answer that closes the question. There is only the ongoing effort of reason, conscience, and character to answer it well.
