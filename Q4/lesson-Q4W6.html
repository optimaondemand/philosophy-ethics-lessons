Morality and God: The Euthyphro Dilemma Revisited
Lesson
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Does natural law require God, and does it collapse into "do whatever God commands"?
In Lesson 1.03 you encountered Plato's Euthyphro dilemma: Is something holy because the gods love it, or do the gods love it because it is holy? In ethical terms, the question becomes: Is something good because God commands it, or does God command it because it is good? This dilemma poses a challenge for any ethics that claims to be grounded in God's will. Natural law theory has a distinctive and sophisticated response to this dilemma. This lesson examines that response, and asks whether natural law needs God at all, or whether it can stand on purely rational grounds.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to restate the Euthyphro dilemma in ethical terms and explain why it poses a challenge for divine command theory.
2
I will be able to explain Aquinas's response: that natural law is grounded in God's rational nature, not in arbitrary divine will.
3
I will be able to explain whether natural law can function without a theological foundation, and what changes if it does.
📚 PART 1 · THE EUTHYPHRO DILEMMA REVISITED
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
In Lesson 1.03 you saw the Euthyphro dilemma for the first time. Now that you have studied three quarters of ethics: do you think morality depends on God, or is it independent of God?

The Dilemma Stated

Recall the dilemma in ethical terms: Is something morally right because God commands it, or does God command it because it is morally right?

  • If right because God commands it (divine command theory): Then morality is arbitrary. God could have commanded cruelty and cruelty would be right. There is no reason why God's commands should be good other than the bare fact that God issued them. Moreover, to call God "good" becomes tautological: God is good because God is good by definition.
  • If God commands it because it is right: Then there is a moral standard independent of and above God's will, to which God's commands must conform. God becomes a reporter of moral truths rather than their author. Morality does not depend on God; God depends on morality.

Both horns seem to create serious problems for any ethics that wants to be both God-grounded and non-arbitrary.

📚 PART 2 · AQUINAS'S RESPONSE

Escaping the Dilemma: God's Rational Nature

Aquinas argues that the Euthyphro dilemma presents a false choice. Both horns assume that God's will is one thing and the moral order is another. Aquinas denies this. For Aquinas, God is not a being with a will who issues arbitrary commands; God is the source of being itself, and God's nature is essentially rational and good. The eternal law is not an arbitrary decree of God's will but the expression of God's reason ordering all things to their ends. The moral order follows from what God is, not from what God arbitrarily decides.

To the objection "God could have made cruelty right by commanding it," Aquinas replies: God could not have commanded cruelty because cruelty is contrary to the good, and God, being essentially good, cannot will the contrary of the good. Asking whether God could have made cruelty right is like asking whether God could have made a triangle have four sides: it is not a limitation on God's power but a recognition that some things are not genuinely possible.

AQUINAS'S RESOLUTION OF THE EUTHYPHRO DILEMMA
The moral order is neither above God nor arbitrarily commanded by God. It is an expression of what God essentially is.
The eternal law is God's reason itself, as the principle governing the order of creation. Natural law is human reason's participation in this order.

Natural Law Without God: The Secular Version

Finnis argues that the basic goods and the requirements of practical reasonableness are knowable by reason alone, without theological premises. A person who believes in no God can still recognize that life, knowledge, and friendship are basic goods, and that it is irrational to act directly against them. This is the "secular natural law" position: natural law's moral conclusions are available to anyone who reasons carefully, regardless of theological commitments.

However, Finnis acknowledges that this secular version faces a question it cannot itself fully answer: why should human beings be rational in this way? If the basic goods are genuinely obligatory, where does that obligation come from? Finnis's own view is that this question ultimately points toward a theological grounding: the obligation is grounded in the rational order of creation, which in turn is grounded in what God is. But the secular version of natural law is a genuine contribution: the moral conclusions can be argued for, examined, and debated independently of any particular religious tradition.

Think about this first, then open: Does Aquinas's resolution of the Euthyphro dilemma succeed? Write or think through your answer before clicking.
The most serious remaining objection: Even if God's nature is necessarily good, how do we determine what God's nature requires for human beings? If our knowledge of God's nature comes from reason reflecting on human nature, then we are back to natural law operating independently. If it comes from revelation, then we need to resolve questions about which revelations are authoritative. Aquinas's response is that these two sources, reason and revelation, are complementary and ultimately consistent: what reason discerns about the good for human beings is confirmed by, not replaced by, what revelation teaches. This "two-source" account is characteristic of the Catholic natural law tradition and was formally articulated at the First Vatican Council (1870).

💪 WHAT YOU HAVE LEARNED

Aquinas's natural law theory escapes the Euthyphro dilemma by identifying a third option: the moral order is neither above God's will (as if God were just reporting pre-existing truths) nor an arbitrary product of God's commands (as divine command theory holds). Instead, the moral order is an expression of what God essentially is: rational, good, and the source of the order of creation. Natural law is human reason's participation in this order. Finnis's secular version shows that the moral conclusions of natural law can be argued for independently of theological premises. The next lesson examines how natural law is known: through reason, conscience, and the common first principles that all human beings share.
