How Morality Is Known: Conscience, Reason, and Synderesis

📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
If natural law is real, how do human beings come to know it?
Natural law theory claims that moral truths are objective and knowable by reason. But this raises a pressing epistemological question: through what faculty or capacity does a human being come to know what natural law requires? Aquinas's answer involves two closely related concepts: synderesis (the innate practical reason that grasps first principles) and conscientia (conscience: the application of those principles to particular cases). Understanding these concepts, and the ways in which moral knowledge can go wrong, is essential to a complete account of natural law theory.

📚 PART 1 · SYNDERESIS: THE SEED OF MORAL KNOWLEDGE
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
When people appeal to "conscience," what do they usually mean? Is conscience an infallible guide, a feeling, a cultural product, or something else?

Synderesis: The Natural Habit of First Principles

Aquinas uses the term synderesis (from the Greek, meaning "preservation" or "watching over") for the natural habit by which human beings grasp the first principles of practical reason. Just as the theoretical intellect has a natural habit of grasping self-evident truths (the principle of non-contradiction, the principle that the whole is greater than any of its parts), the practical intellect has a natural habit of grasping the first principles of action: do good and avoid evil; preserve life; seek truth; live in community.

Synderesis is infallible with respect to these first principles: no human being who is reasoning at all can genuinely fail to grasp that good is to be done and evil avoided. It can be confused, suppressed, or displaced by passion or habit, but it cannot be destroyed. Aquinas uses the image of a spark: the scintilla conscientiae, the spark of conscience, which remains even in the most corrupt person and can, in principle, be rekindled.

AQUINAS'S TWO-LEVEL ACCOUNT OF MORAL KNOWLEDGE
Synderesis (Level 1)
The natural habit of first principles.
Grasps self-evident first principles of action: do good, avoid evil; preserve life; seek truth. These principles are universal and cannot be erased.
Conscientia (Level 2)
The application of principles to particular cases.
Applies first principles to specific situations: is this particular act one of the kind prohibited? Conscientia can be mistaken; it requires reasoning, and reasoning can go wrong.
📚 PART 2 · ERRONEOUS CONSCIENCE

Can Conscience Be Wrong?

Aquinas holds that conscience (at the level of conscientia) can be mistaken, and often is. The error typically arises not at the level of first principles (synderesis) but in the application of those principles to particular cases: a person may correctly grasp that innocent people should not be harmed but make an error in judging whether a particular person is innocent, or whether a particular action constitutes harm. Errors can also arise from false beliefs about what human nature requires, from disordered passions that distort judgment, or from cultural conditioning that misidentifies goods.

Erroneous Conscience Still Binds

One of Aquinas's most striking claims is that even an erroneous conscience binds: if you genuinely believe in good conscience that an action is right, you are obligated to do it (and vice versa). This may seem paradoxical. If the action is actually wrong, how can you be obligated to do it? Aquinas's answer is that conscience is the proximate rule of moral action: you must act according to your best judgment of what reason requires, not according to some standard you cannot access. A person who acts against his own conscience is acting badly, even if his conscience happens to be misinformed.

However, Aquinas adds a critical qualification: you are obligated to form your conscience well. If your conscience is erroneous because of vincible ignorance (ignorance you could have corrected if you had taken reasonable steps to find out the truth), you are morally responsible for the error. Only invincible ignorance (ignorance that no reasonable effort could have dispelled) fully excuses the person who acts on an erroneous conscience.

Think about this first, then open: If erroneous conscience still binds, does this mean natural law theory collapses into moral relativism? Write your answer before clicking.
No, and here is why. The claim that erroneous conscience binds is a claim about subjective moral responsibility, not about objective moral truth. The person who acts on an erroneous conscience is not doing what is objectively right; she is doing what she genuinely and non-culpably believes to be right. Natural law theory maintains that there are objective moral truths (grounded in the basic goods and in natural law) and that these truths do not depend on what any individual or culture believes. The doctrine of erroneous conscience is about how we assess the moral responsibility of a person given her epistemic situation, not about whether the objective moral order varies from person to person. The two levels are distinct: objective moral truth is independent; subjective moral responsibility depends on what you know and what you could reasonably have known.

💪 WHAT YOU HAVE LEARNED

Natural law is known through two levels of practical reason: synderesis (the natural habit that grasps self-evident first principles of action) and conscientia (the application of those principles to particular cases). Synderesis is infallible at the level of first principles; conscientia can be mistaken. Erroneous conscience still binds at the level of subjective responsibility, but this does not collapse into relativism: the objective moral order remains independent of what any person or culture believes.
