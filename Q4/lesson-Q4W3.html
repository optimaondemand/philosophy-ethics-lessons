The Basic Human Goods: What Natural Law Tells Us to Pursue

📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
What are the basic goods that all human beings seek, and how does natural reason identify them?
If the first precept of natural law is "do good and avoid evil," the content of that precept depends on identifying what the basic human goods are. Aquinas identifies them by looking at the natural inclinations of human beings: the goods toward which human beings are naturally ordered by their nature. The contemporary natural law philosophers Germain Grisez and John Finnis (in Natural Law and Natural Rights, 1980) developed this Thomistic account into a full theory of "basic goods": a set of fundamental goods that are self-evidently valuable, not derivable from each other, and not reducible to any single master value.

2

Three Tiers of Natural Inclination

Aquinas identifies three levels of natural inclination, each generating a set of natural law precepts:

Level Shared with Inclination Natural law precept
1. Shared with all substances All existing things To preserve its own being and integrity Preserve human life; do not destroy it without justification
2. Shared with animals All animals Procreation and care of offspring; social living Protect and nurture the family; care for children
3. Proper to rational beings Human beings only To know truth (including about God); to live in community; to act according to reason Seek truth; cultivate knowledge; live justly in community
📚 PART 2 · THE BASIC GOODS: FINNIS AND GRISEZ

Finnis's Catalogue of Basic Goods

John Finnis (born 1940), the most influential contemporary natural law philosopher, systematized Aquinas's account in Natural Law and Natural Rights (1980). He identifies seven basic goods, which he argues are: (1) self-evident, not in need of further justification; (2) incommensurable with each other, not reducible to a single master value; and (3) each a reason for action in its own right.

Life
Health, vitality, and freedom from injury. The goods of bodily integrity that make all other goods accessible. This includes one's own life and, by extension, a concern for the lives of others.

Knowledge
Truth pursued for its own sake, not merely as a means to practical success. The natural curiosity of the mind, the desire to understand reality as it is. Finnis includes speculative and practical knowledge.

Play
Engaging in activities for the pure enjoyment of them, without any further purpose. This is not frivolity but the expression of a dimension of human freedom: the capacity to do something for itself alone.

Aesthetic Experience
The enjoyment of beauty in art and nature. The capacity to appreciate form, harmony, and excellence in created things. This is distinct from both pleasure and knowledge: it is the contemplative response to beauty.

Sociability (Friendship)
The good of genuine friendship and community: being with others in relationships of mutual concern and affection. Finnis's account here draws directly on Aristotle's account of philia from NE Books VIII-IX.

Practical Reasonableness
The good of using one's reason effectively in shaping one's own life and decisions. This is not merely intelligence but the integrated exercise of freedom, reason, and will in ordering one's actions and commitments.

Religion
The good of seeking a right relationship to whatever transcends human existence: the ultimate source of order and value. For Finnis, this is a basic human good even from a purely philosophical standpoint, because the question of ultimate origin and meaning is inescapable for rational beings.

Incommensurability and Its Consequences

The most philosophically important feature of the basic goods account is that the basic goods are incommensurable: they cannot be ranked or traded off against each other by reference to some common scale of value. There is no master currency into which life, knowledge, friendship, and practical reasonableness can all be converted. This has a decisive implication: you cannot justify destroying a basic good (acting directly against it) by appealing to a greater gain in the same or another basic good. Directly harming an innocent person to produce a better outcome for others is not permissible, because there is no common scale that makes this comparison legitimate.

This is natural law theory's structural response to the consequentialist justification of using persons as means: the problem is not merely that it feels wrong, but that it involves a conceptual error. The claim that "the harm to this person is outweighed by the good to the others" presupposes that the goods of different persons can be aggregated on a single scale. Natural law theory denies this.

📚 PART 3 · RECOGNIZING THE BASIC GOODS
⚡ TRY IT · WHICH BASIC GOOD?
Identify which of Finnis's basic goods is most directly at stake in each scenario.
1. A student stays up late reading about the history of ancient civilizations, not because it is required for school, but because he finds it genuinely fascinating and wants to understand it.
2. Two friends make a deliberate effort to maintain their friendship across distance, meeting regularly and being honest with each other even when honesty is uncomfortable.
3. A doctor declines to perform an elective procedure that carries significant risk without commensurate benefit, in order to protect the patient's health and bodily integrity.

💪 WHAT YOU HAVE LEARNED

Natural law theory identifies a set of basic human goods by reflecting on the natural inclinations of human beings: goods toward which human nature is ordered across three levels (shared with all substances, shared with animals, proper to rational beings). Finnis systematizes this into seven basic goods: life, knowledge, play, aesthetic experience, sociability, practical reasonableness, and religion. These goods are incommensurable: they cannot be ranked or traded on a single scale of value. This incommensurability explains why directly acting against a basic good cannot be justified by appeal to better consequences. The next lesson examines what moral obligation means in this framework: why the basic goods are not merely desirable but obligatory.
