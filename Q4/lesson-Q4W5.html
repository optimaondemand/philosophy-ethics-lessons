📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
What is morality, and why is it not reducible to social convention, personal preference, or the commands of the powerful?
Through three quarters you have studied four ethical theories and the objections to each. This lesson steps back from the level of specific theories and asks the more fundamental question: what is the domain of morality itself? Why do moral questions deserve serious answers at all? Natural law theory offers a distinctive answer: morality is the set of requirements that follow from reason's grasp of the good for human beings. This is not merely a cultural construction, not a matter of taste, and not simply the preferences of whoever holds power. Understanding why natural law makes this case, and what makes it compelling, is the goal of this lesson.

I will be able to explain three rival accounts of what morality is and articulate the natural law account against them.
2

📚 PART 1 · RIVAL ACCOUNTS OF MORALITY
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
Before reading on: is morality a matter of fact, a matter of preference, or a matter of social agreement? Does your answer change for different moral questions?

Three Rival Accounts of Morality

Moral Relativism
Moral claims are true only relative to a culture, society, or individual. There are no universal moral truths. What is right in one society may be wrong in another, and there is no objective basis for judging between them.
Problem: This cannot explain why we judge some historical practices (slavery, genocide) as wrong regardless of cultural approval, or why moral progress is possible.
Legal Positivism
Morality is what the law commands, or what the state declares. There is no independent moral standard above positive law. H.L.A. Hart and early Austin held versions of this view.
Problem: This cannot account for judgments that laws themselves can be unjust, or for the Nuremberg principle that "following orders" is no defense for serious crimes.
Natural Law (Aquinas/Finnis)
Morality is the set of requirements that follow from reason's grasp of the basic human goods. These are universal, knowable by reason, and binding regardless of cultural agreement or positive law.
This account can explain both why moral progress is possible (we come to understand the basic goods better) and why some things are wrong regardless of cultural approval.
📚 PART 2 · THE DOCTRINE OF DOUBLE EFFECT

A Critical Tool: Distinguishing What We Intend from What We Foresee

Natural law theory holds that it is never permissible to act directly against a basic good. But many actions have harmful effects as foreseen side effects, without those harms being the point of the action. A surgeon who removes a cancerous uterus foresees that the patient will lose the ability to bear children, but does not intend this as the goal of the surgery. A soldier defending a city foresees that civilians may be killed by the return fire, but does not intend to kill them.

The Doctrine of Double Effect (DDE) is the natural law principle that distinguishes morally between an action's intended effects and its merely foreseen effects. It originates in Aquinas (ST II-II, Q. 64, A. 7, on self-defense) and has been refined extensively in the centuries since.

THE FOUR CONDITIONS OF THE DOCTRINE OF DOUBLE EFFECT
1
The action itself must not be intrinsically evil
The action being performed must not itself be a direct violation of natural law. You cannot do an evil thing in order to bring about a good result.
2
The agent must intend only the good effect
The harmful effect must be foreseen but not intended. It must not be the goal or the means to the goal. The agent acts in spite of the harmful effect, not because of it.
3
The good effect must not flow from the bad effect
The good result must not be achieved by means of the harmful effect. If the good follows causally from the harm (the good result is caused by the harm), then the harm is being used as a means, which is prohibited.
4
There must be proportionate reason
The good achieved must be sufficiently serious to justify permitting the harmful side effect. You cannot accept significant harm to secure a trivial benefit.

All four conditions must be satisfied. If any one fails, the action is not permissible under the DDE.

💪 WHAT YOU HAVE LEARNED

Natural law theory holds that morality is the set of requirements that follow from reason's grasp of the basic human goods. It rejects moral relativism (morality as cultural convention) and legal positivism (morality as what the law commands). The Doctrine of Double Effect is its central tool for distinguishing permissible from impermissible actions that involve harmful effects: an action is permissible only if (1) the action itself is not intrinsically wrong, (2) the harm is foreseen but not intended, (3) the good does not flow from the harm, and (4) there is proportionate reason.
