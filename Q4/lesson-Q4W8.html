📚 PART 1 · THE LOGICAL STRUCTURE OF NATURAL LAW THEORY
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
Before reading on: in your own words, what is the most important philosophical contribution of natural law theory to the ethics you have studied this year?

The Argument in Five Steps

THE CHAIN OF ARGUMENT IN NATURAL LAW THEORY
1
There is a moral order built into nature. Human beings have a nature, and that nature has a telos: an end toward which it is ordered. The moral order follows from what human beings are and what they are for.
2
The first principle is: do good and avoid evil. Good is what human beings are naturally oriented toward. Evil is the privation of good. This principle structures all practical reasoning.
3
The basic human goods provide the content. Life, knowledge, friendship, practical reasonableness, and the other basic goods are what human nature is ordered toward. They are incommensurable: no common scale exists to trade them off against each other.
4
Absolute prohibitions follow. It is always wrong to directly act against a basic good. No consequence calculation can override this, because the incommensurability of the basic goods rules out the calculation.
5
Human law must conform to natural law. Valid human law derives its authority from conformity to the natural moral order. An unjust law is no law at all.
📚 PART 2 · PRINCIPAL OBJECTIONS AND RESPONSES

Objection 1: The Is-Ought Gap

Objection: You cannot derive an "ought" from an "is." Even if human beings have a nature and that nature has a telos, this is a description of what is. No amount of description tells us what ought to be done. Hume's is-ought gap shows that natural facts about human inclinations and ends cannot by themselves generate moral obligations.

Natural law response: Natural law theory does not derive "ought" from "is" in the way Hume describes. The first principle of natural law ("do good and avoid evil") is not derived from a description of human inclinations; it is grasped by practical reason as a self-evident truth. What the account of natural inclinations does is identify the content of what "good" means for human beings: not what to pursue because humans happen to pursue it, but what is genuinely good for beings of this nature. The argument from natural inclinations is not a derivation but a disclosure of what reason already recognizes as good.

Objection 2: Cultural Variation

Objection: Different cultures have radically different moral codes. If natural law were universal and knowable by reason, we would expect much more agreement. The disagreement itself is evidence against universality.

Natural law response: Two points. First, the variation in moral codes is often overstated: at the level of first principles (do not kill the innocent, care for children, tell the truth, live in community), the agreement across cultures is remarkably broad. What varies is usually the application of these principles to particular cases: who counts as innocent, what forms of lying are permissible, and so on. Second, variation in belief does not entail variation in truth. Mathematical errors are widespread; we do not conclude that mathematical truths vary by culture.

Objection 3: The Naturalistic Fallacy

Objection: G.E. Moore (1873–1958) argued in Principia Ethica that any attempt to define "good" in terms of natural properties (pleasure, flourishing, what we are inclined toward) commits the "naturalistic fallacy": confusing what is good with what is natural. For any natural property you name, it remains an open question whether it is good.

Natural law response: Finnis argues that Moore's objection misidentifies the structure of the natural law account. The basic goods are not identified as good because they are natural; they are identified as good because practical reason grasps them as self-evidently valuable. The appeal to natural inclinations is a heuristic for focusing attention on the goods that practical reason will then recognize; it is not itself the justification. The justification is the self-evidence of the basic goods to any person who reasons carefully.

💪 WHAT YOU HAVE LEARNED

Natural law theory grounds morality in human nature and its telos, identifies the basic human goods through which that nature is fulfilled, derives absolute prohibitions from the incommensurability of those goods, and locates moral knowledge in synderesis and conscience. Its three principal objections (the is-ought gap, cultural variation, and the naturalistic fallacy) have carefully developed responses. It incorporates insights from both Kantian deontology (the moral significance of intention) and virtue ethics (the role of character in reliable moral action). The final lesson synthesizes the entire course.
