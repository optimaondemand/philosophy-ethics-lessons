Nature and Moral Order: Is There a Law Written into Reality?
Lesson
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Is there a moral order built into nature itself, or do human beings make up morality from scratch?
Natural law theory is the oldest and most influential tradition in Western ethics. It holds that human reason can discern, from the structure of human nature and reality itself, basic principles of how human beings ought to act. These principles are not invented by legislatures, not discovered in religious texts alone, and not relative to cultural preference. They are discovered by reason as it reflects on what human beings are and what they are for. Quarter 4 develops this tradition from its ancient roots through its mature expression in Aquinas and into its contemporary relevance. The central text for the quarter is Thomas Aquinas (1225–1274), whose Summa Theologica contains the definitive classical account of natural law.

📚 PART 1 · THE CENTRAL CLAIM
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
Do you believe that some things are morally wrong regardless of what any government, culture, or religion says about them? If yes, on what basis are they wrong?

The Core Claim

Natural law theory holds that there is a moral order that is objective, discoverable by reason, and grounded in the nature of things rather than in human convention. This claim has three components:

  1. Objective: Moral truths are not invented or decided by human beings; they hold regardless of what any individual or community believes about them.
  2. Discoverable by reason: Moral truths can be known through the natural capacity of human reason to reflect on what human beings are and what they are for. This knowledge does not require revelation, though it is consistent with it.
  3. Grounded in nature: The source of moral truths is the nature of things, above all human nature and its telos (end or purpose). Because human beings have a nature, there are things that fulfill it and things that frustrate it.
📚 PART 2 · THE TRADITION: ARISTOTLE, CICERO, AQUINAS

Aristotle: The Teleological Foundation

The ancient roots of natural law lie in Aristotle's teleological account of nature. For Aristotle, every natural kind has a telos: an end or function that defines what it means for a thing of that kind to be excellent. An acorn's telos is to become an oak; a human being's telos is to exercise the soul excellently in accordance with reason. Moral norms follow from this: what is genuinely good for human beings is what enables them to achieve their telos. Aristotle also distinguishes, in the Rhetoric, between laws that apply "to us only" and laws "common to all," the latter grounded in nature rather than human convention.

Cicero: Natural Law as Universal Reason

The Roman statesman and philosopher Marcus Tullius Cicero (106–43 BC) gave natural law its most eloquent ancient formulation. In De Re Publica he wrote: "True law is right reason in agreement with nature. It is of universal application, unchanging and everlasting; it summons to duty by its commands, and averts from wrongdoing by its prohibitions... We cannot be freed from its obligations by Senate or People, and we need not look outside ourselves for an expounder or interpreter of it." Cicero's formulation is important for two reasons: it identifies natural law with right reason rather than with mere biology, and it insists on universality as the test of natural law's validity.

Aquinas: The Four-Tier Hierarchy of Law

Thomas Aquinas synthesized the Aristotelian and Ciceronian traditions, integrating them with the Christian theological tradition. In Summa Theologica I-II, Questions 90–94, he articulates the definitive classical account. Aquinas distinguishes four types of law that form a hierarchy:

AQUINAS'S FOUR-TIER HIERARCHY OF LAW (ST I-II, QQ. 90-97)
1
Eternal Law
The rational order by which God governs all creation. It is the original source of all law. Human beings cannot know the eternal law in its fullness, but they participate in it through reason.
2
Divine Law
God's explicit commands as revealed in Scripture. It supplements natural law by providing guidance beyond what unaided reason can reach, particularly regarding humanity's supernatural end.
3
Natural Law (our focus this quarter)
Human reason's participation in the eternal law. The basic principles of right action that reason discerns from human nature: seek the good and avoid evil, preserve human life, live in community, seek truth. These precepts are universal and knowable by all rational persons.
4
Human Law
Particular determinations made by human authority: statutes, regulations, constitutions. These are valid only insofar as they are consistent with natural law. An unjust human law is no law at all.

The Key Claim: Human Law Must Conform to Natural Law

For Aquinas, human-made law is not self-validating. A law that commands something contrary to the natural moral order is not a genuine law but a corruption of law. This is the principle that later grounded resistance to unjust regimes: during the Nuremberg Trials (which you examined in Q2), the defense that soldiers were merely following legal orders was rejected precisely on natural law grounds. The judgment affirmed that there is a moral order above positive law to which legal systems are accountable.

💪 WHAT YOU HAVE LEARNED

Natural law theory holds that there is a moral order that is objective, discoverable by reason, and grounded in the nature of things. It has its roots in Aristotle's teleological ethics and Cicero's account of universal reason, and reaches its mature expression in Aquinas's four-tier hierarchy of law. For Aquinas, natural law is human reason's participation in the rational order governing all creation. Human law is valid only insofar as it conforms to natural law. The next lesson examines what natural law theory means by "the good" and why the first precept of natural law is: do good and avoid evil.
