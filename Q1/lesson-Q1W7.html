Does Evolution Explain Morality? The Limits of Evolutionary Ethics
Lesson
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
If our moral instincts evolved because they helped our ancestors survive, does that make them true — or merely useful?
Since Darwin, thinkers have asked whether evolutionary biology could explain — and thereby ground — human moral behavior. This approach is taken seriously by contemporary scientists and philosophers. This lesson examines what evolutionary accounts of morality can genuinely explain, and where they hit a philosophical wall.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to explain the basic evolutionary account of moral behavior and what it claims to explain.
2
I will be able to explain the naturalistic fallacy and why an evolutionary origin for moral beliefs does not validate them.
3

📚 PART 1 · THE EVOLUTIONARY ACCOUNT
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
If you discovered that your tendency to feel that fairness matters evolved because it helped your ancestors cooperate and survive, would that change how seriously you take that feeling morally?

Darwin's Descent of Man and Social Instincts

In The Descent of Man (1871), Charles Darwin proposed that moral behavior in humans evolved from the social instincts of earlier species. Animals that lived in groups developed instincts for cooperation, mutual aid, and social cohesion — because groups with these traits survived better than those without. In human beings, these social instincts became the foundation of the moral sense. Darwin observed that animals exhibit what appears to be sympathy, concern for offspring, and loyalty to the group — rudimentary versions of what would become moral feeling in humans.

Later thinkers developed this line of thought. Herbert Spencer coined the phrase "survival of the fittest" and argued that evolution provided a natural standard for ethics: whatever promotes survival and flourishing is good. In the twentieth century, sociobiology and evolutionary psychology produced increasingly sophisticated accounts of how altruism, fairness, reciprocity, and kin preference might all have evolutionary explanations.

What Evolutionary Ethics Can Explain

The evolutionary account has real explanatory power at the level of why we have the moral feelings we have. The near-universality of prohibitions on in-group killing, obligations to care for children, and reciprocity norms is exactly what evolutionary theory would predict, if cooperative social behavior promotes group survival. Evolutionary psychology illuminates why moral intuitions feel compelling and immediate, rather than derived from reasoning. In that sense, it explains the psychology of morality.

📚 PART 2 · WHERE EVOLUTIONARY ETHICS CANNOT GO

The Naturalistic Fallacy

The philosopher G.E. Moore identified what he called the naturalistic fallacy: the error of defining "good" in terms of any natural property, including properties identified by science. Spencer's error was precisely this: he argued that because evolution produces certain traits, those traits are good. But this is a version of Hume's is/ought gap. The fact that evolution selected for a trait does not make the trait morally valuable. Evolution has also selected for tribalism, violence against outgroups, deception, and cheating when profitable. If "good" means "selected by evolution," then all of these would also be good.

Moreover, evolution has no goal. Natural selection does not "aim" at flourishing; it selects for reproductive fitness. A trait that maximized reproduction at the cost of every other aspect of well-being would be evolutionarily selected. If evolution were our moral guide, we would have to follow wherever reproductive fitness leads — a conclusion virtually no one is willing to accept.

The Origin/Justification Distinction

There is a crucial distinction between the origin of a belief and its justification. The origin of our belief in arithmetic may be that counting helped our ancestors track resources. That evolutionary origin does not make arithmetic true or false; arithmetic is justified on its own terms. The same applies to moral beliefs. Even if our moral instincts evolved, they may still accurately track something real about human flourishing, obligation, and the good. The evolutionary account explains how we came to have these beliefs; it does not settle whether the beliefs are correct.

Naturalistic fallacy (G.E. Moore): The error of defining "good" in terms of any natural property. Applied here: the error of concluding that because evolution selected for a trait, the trait is morally good.

Origin vs. justification: The cause of a belief (how you came to hold it) is distinct from its justification (whether you have good reasons to hold it). Evolutionary origin does not settle the question of moral justification.

💪 WHAT YOU HAVE LEARNED

Evolution explains why we have the moral instincts we have. It does not make those instincts correct, any more than the evolutionary origin of our number sense makes arithmetic true. The philosophical question — which moral claims are genuinely justified, and on what grounds — is not answered by biology. It requires the kind of reasoning that the rest of this course develops.
