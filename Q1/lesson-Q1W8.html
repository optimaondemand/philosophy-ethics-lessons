What Does the Brain Tell Us About Right and Wrong? Psychology, Neuroscience, and Moral Judgment
Lesson
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Neuroscientists can now observe the brain during moral decisions. Does that tell us what morality is?
Recent decades have produced a wave of scientific research on the psychology and neuroscience of moral judgment. Researchers can identify which brain regions activate when people face moral dilemmas, measure how quickly moral judgments are made, and observe how emotion and cognition interact in moral decision-making. This lesson examines what this research genuinely contributes to our understanding of moral life — and why it does not, by itself, resolve the philosophical questions this course is asking.

📚 PART 1 · WHAT THE SCIENCE SHOWS
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
Think about the last time you made a moral judgment quickly, without deliberation. Was that judgment more like a perception, a calculation, or a gut feeling?

Jonathan Haidt and Moral Intuition

In a widely discussed 2001 paper in Psychological Review, Jonathan Haidt proposed the social intuitionist model of moral judgment. Haidt's research showed that people make moral judgments rapidly, before any deliberate reasoning, and then construct post-hoc rationalizations for those judgments. On Haidt's account, moral reasoning is largely a performance — we have already decided what we think based on gut feeling, and reasoning is deployed to persuade others rather than to reach conclusions. He summarized the position as: "The moral dog wags its rational tail."

Neuroimaging studies have supported parts of this picture. The brain regions most active during rapid moral judgments include areas associated with emotion (the amygdala, the ventromedial prefrontal cortex). When people face trolley-problem-style dilemmas that require deliberate calculation of consequences, different regions of the prefrontal cortex become active, and responses are slower. The neuroscience suggests that moral judgment involves at least two different kinds of processing: fast emotional response and slower deliberative reasoning.

What This Research Illuminates

This research is genuinely valuable for understanding how moral judgments are made. It confirms the sentimentalist point that emotion is central to moral experience. It explains why moral argument is often less effective than moral education and habituation at changing behavior — something Aristotle insisted on two and a half millennia ago. And it raises important questions about the reliability of moral intuitions that have been formed in environments very different from the ones we now navigate.

📚 PART 2 · WHAT SCIENCE CANNOT SETTLE

The Descriptive/Normative Gap, Again

Neuroscience tells us how moral judgments are processed in the brain. It does not tell us which moral judgments are correct. The brain also processes judgments that are morally false: biases, prejudices, and rationalizations all have neural correlates. The mere fact that a judgment activates certain brain regions does not make it right.

More fundamentally: even if Haidt is right that moral reasoning often post-hoc rationalizes intuition, this does not show that moral reasoning is worthless. It shows that human beings sometimes reason badly. The capacity to reason can be exercised well or poorly. When Socrates questioned Euthyphro, he was not merely post-hoc rationalizing — he was genuinely testing whether an intuition survived scrutiny. The existence of bad reasoning does not invalidate good reasoning.

Finally, the neuroscientific account simply pushes the question back. Once we know that moral judgment involves emotional and deliberative processing, we still need to ask: which of these processes is morally reliable? Should we trust our quick emotional reactions or our considered deliberations? That question is a philosophical one, and no amount of brain imaging can answer it.

Social intuitionist model (Haidt): The psychological account on which moral judgments are made rapidly by intuition, with deliberate reasoning typically serving to justify the conclusion already reached.

Neuroethics: The interdisciplinary field applying neuroscientific findings to ethical questions, both by illuminating how moral judgment works and by raising new ethical questions about the brain itself.

💪 WHAT YOU HAVE LEARNED

Psychology and neuroscience have made real contributions to our understanding of moral psychology: they confirm the importance of emotion, illuminate the speed and automaticity of moral judgment, and raise important questions about reliability. But they do not, and cannot, resolve the normative question: which moral judgments are correct, and why. That question requires the kind of reasoning this course has been building toward.
