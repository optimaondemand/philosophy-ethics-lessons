Is There a Fact About What Is Good? The Fact/Value Distinction and Its Limits
Lesson
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Can you logically derive an "ought" from an "is"? And what happens to ethics if you cannot?
Emotivism and moral relativism both assume a sharp distinction between facts and values: facts are knowable and objective; values are merely subjective. This assumption drives a philosophical challenge to the very possibility of ethics. This lesson examines the challenge at its root, considers the most serious attempt to press it (Friedrich Nietzsche's critique), and explains why the challenge, though powerful, ultimately fails.

📚 PART 1 · HUME'S CHALLENGE: THE IS/OUGHT GAP
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
Consider this argument: "Human beings are social animals who depend on one another. Therefore, we ought to help each other." Does the conclusion follow from the premise? (No wrong answer.)

Hume's Observation

David Hume (1711–1776), the Scottish philosopher, noticed something troubling in the moral writing of his day. Authors would begin with statements about how things are — facts about human nature, history, or psychology — and then, without any explicit justification, slide into statements about what ought to be done. Hume called this transition illegitimate. An "ought," he argued, cannot be derived from an "is." No collection of purely factual statements, however large, logically entails a normative conclusion. The gap between description and prescription is absolute.

If Hume is right, the implications for ethics are significant. Values cannot be read off the natural world. They cannot be verified by science. They seem to vary across cultures and periods. Two conclusions tend to follow. The first is relativism: if moral claims report no objective facts, perhaps they report facts about us — what we, as individuals or cultures, happen to approve. The second is moral skepticism: if moral claims cannot be grounded in anything objective, perhaps they have no claim to truth at all. This is the position sometimes called moral nihilism: the view that there are no moral facts, and statements like "cruelty is wrong" are neither true nor false — they merely express attitudes, like an exclamation of disgust.

Nietzsche: The Challenge to Moral Tradition

Friedrich Nietzsche (1844–1900) pressed the challenge to conventional morality in a more radical direction than any philosopher before him. He did not merely argue that moral claims lack objective grounding. He argued that conventional moral systems — particularly those shaped by the Christian tradition — rest on unexamined assumptions that, when exposed, prove to be something other than disinterested pursuit of the good. In works such as Beyond Good and Evil, Nietzsche subjected inherited moral norms to genealogical scrutiny, asking not "is this true?" but "who benefits from believing this?" and "what kind of person does this morality produce?"

Nietzsche's critique has genuine philosophical weight in one respect: he correctly identified that moral assumptions often go unexamined, that the comfortable assumption that one's own moral tradition is obviously right is a form of intellectual laziness, and that a serious thinker should be willing to ask hard questions about the foundations of any moral system. This is, in fact, exactly what Socrates was doing in the Euthyphro. The Socratic spirit and the Nietzschean spirit share a willingness to question received wisdom.

Where Nietzsche goes wrong — and where his critique ultimately self-destructs — is in the move from "these moral assumptions deserve to be questioned" to "there are no moral truths at all." That move does not follow. The fact that a moral tradition can be questioned is not evidence that morality has no rational foundation; it is evidence that the foundation has not yet been made fully explicit. That is an invitation to do better philosophy, not to abandon the enterprise.

THE FACT/VALUE DISTINCTION: WHAT IT SHOWS AND WHAT IT DOES NOT
What it correctly shows
You cannot derive "we ought to X" from "humans do X" by logic alone. Factual premises need normative premises to generate normative conclusions.
What it does not show
That facts about human nature are morally irrelevant, or that no moral claim is objectively true, or that moral reasoning is impossible. The gap only shows that moral reasoning requires both factual and normative premises.
📚 PART 2 · THE CASE AGAINST MORAL SKEPTICISM

Three Responses to the Challenge

The philosophical tradition has developed several powerful responses to the moral skepticism that the fact/value distinction seems to generate. Three deserve careful attention.

1. The Naturalist Response (Aristotle)

Aristotle argued in the Nicomachean Ethics that the is/ought gap, taken as absolute, depends on an impoverished account of what a fact is. Facts about a thing's nature entail facts about what counts as its flourishing. A knife is a good knife if it cuts well; that is what knives are for. Human beings have a characteristic way of living and functioning — a nature — and facts about that nature bear directly on what constitutes their genuine good. Hume's gap is only absolute if you assume that facts about human nature are morally inert. That assumption, Aristotle would say, is precisely what is in question.

2. The Self-Refutation Response

Moral skepticism — the view that there are no moral truths — faces the same self-refutation problem as moral relativism. To argue that moral claims are not objectively true, the moral skeptic must use reason, present evidence, and appeal to standards of logical validity. But these activities presuppose that some claims are better than others — that the argument for moral skepticism is itself better supported than the alternative. This is itself a normative judgment. The moment you argue for moral skepticism, you are already making a judgment that functions exactly like a moral claim. The position is unstable.

3. The Moral Perception Response

Several philosophers in the Aristotelian tradition — Philippa Foot, Alasdair MacIntyre — have argued that our confidence in basic moral claims is often stronger than our confidence in the philosophical theories that deny them. We know that gratuitous cruelty is wrong with a clarity that outstrips most scientific and philosophical conclusions. A theory that implies otherwise has not refuted our moral knowledge; it has simply shown that the theory needs revision. The appropriate response to a philosophical argument that concludes "cruelty is not really wrong" is not to accept the conclusion, but to look for the false premise.

Key Terms

The is/ought gap (Hume): The observation that no normative conclusion ("ought") follows logically from purely descriptive premises ("is") alone, without the addition of further normative premises.

Moral skepticism: The view that there are no objective moral truths, or that moral knowledge is impossible. The strongest form holds that moral statements are neither true nor false.

Genealogical critique: Nietzsche's method of examining the historical origins and psychological motivations of moral beliefs, rather than asking whether they are rationally justified.

💪 WHAT YOU HAVE LEARNED

The fact/value distinction captures a genuine insight: moral conclusions cannot be read off empirical facts the way distances can be measured. But the insight is weaker than it looks. Hume's gap does not show that moral reasoning is impossible — only that moral reasoning requires both factual and normative premises. The challenge to find secure normative premises is real and serious, but it is an invitation to rigorous philosophy, not a reason to abandon the enterprise. By the time you finish this course, you will have a much better sense of where those secure premises can be found.
