📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
What is ethics, and why does it matter that we study it carefully?
You make ethical judgments every day. You decide that some actions are wrong, others admirable, others neutral. This course asks a deeper question: are those judgments reliable? Can they be justified by reasons, or are they just feelings dressed up in serious-sounding language? Before you can answer that, you need to understand what ethics actually is and why it is the kind of thing that can be studied at all.

📚 PART 1 · A MAN WHO KNEW HE WAS RIGHT
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN · Before you read, think about this.

The Porch of the King Archon

In the early fifth century BCE, an Athenian named Euthyphro met Socrates outside the office of the King Archon, the magistrate responsible for religious matters. Euthyphro had come to file a murder charge against his own father. A laborer had killed a slave on his father's estate; the father had bound the man and left him in a ditch while he sent for advice from Athens. The man died before the messenger returned. Euthyphro was convinced that his duty to the gods required him to prosecute, even against his own family. His relatives thought the whole enterprise outrageous. Euthyphro was unmoved. He was certain.

Socrates found this confidence extraordinary. He asked Euthyphro a simple question: what, exactly, is piety? What makes an action holy? Euthyphro offered several answers. Each time, Socrates showed that the answer either described an example without defining the thing, or contained a contradiction. By the end of the dialogue, Euthyphro excused himself and left. The definition remained unfound.

The Euthyphro is the first text of Western ethics for a reason. It is not primarily a discussion of piety. It is a demonstration that confident moral certainty, taken alone, is not the same thing as moral knowledge. Euthyphro knew perfectly well what he intended to do. What he could not do was explain, in terms that held up to questioning, why it was right. That gap — between conviction and justification — is where ethics begins.

THE CENTRAL QUESTION OF THIS COURSE
"Can we know what is right — and can we explain why?"
Euthyphro was sure. Socrates wanted to know the reason. Ethics is the systematic attempt to answer that demand.
📚 PART 2 · WHAT ETHICS IS

Descriptive and Normative Claims

Before you can study ethics, you need to understand what kind of statement an ethical claim is. Consider these two sentences:

(A) "In Athens in 399 BCE, a jury voted to execute Socrates."

(B) "The jury that executed Socrates acted unjustly."

Sentence (A) is a descriptive claim: it reports a fact about the world. It could be verified by historical records. It makes no judgment about whether what happened was good or bad. Sentence (B) is a normative claim: it evaluates the event. It asserts that there is a standard — justice — by which the jury's action falls short.

Ethics is the branch of philosophy that deals with normative claims about human action: what we ought to do, what we ought to avoid, what makes a life well-lived, and what makes an action right or wrong. The word comes from the Greek ethos, meaning character or custom — a reminder that for the Greeks, ethics was not primarily about rules, but about the kind of person one becomes.

When Does an Ethical Question Arise?

Not every question is an ethical question. "What is the boiling point of water?" is a scientific question. "Which route is fastest?" is a practical question. Ethical questions are different. They arise when:

  • An action affects others, and a standard of how they ought to be treated is at stake.
  • A choice involves competing values, and we must decide which takes priority.
  • We are asked to judge whether an action, practice, or character is good or bad, right or wrong.

Notice that ethical questions require a standard. Euthyphro was certain that prosecuting his father was right. Socrates' question forced him to name the standard he was appealing to. That is what ethics does: it makes our standards explicit, examines them, and asks whether they hold up.

Key Terms

Descriptive claim: A statement about how things are. Verifiable in principle by observation or record.

Normative claim: A statement about how things ought to be, or about what is good or right. Appeals to a standard beyond mere fact.

Ethics (from Greek ethos, character): The branch of philosophy that studies normative claims about human action and character. Ethics asks what we ought to do and why.

“The good” (Greek: to agathon): That which is beneficial, excellent, or worth pursuing. Ethics ultimately tries to understand what the good is and how to pursue it.

📚 PART 3 · WHY ETHICS CAN BE STUDIED

The Possibility of Ethical Guidance

Some people assume that because ethics deals with values rather than measurements, it cannot be studied seriously. This assumption is worth challenging at the start. Consider: we regularly judge that some moral arguments are better than others, that some ethical views are more carefully reasoned, and that people can learn to reason about moral questions more carefully. These observations suggest that ethical claims are not simply arbitrary.

Plato's Socrates staked his life on this conviction. He did not claim to have all the answers. He claimed that the questions could be pursued systematically, that better and worse answers could be distinguished, and that a life spent examining these questions was more genuinely human than a life of comfortable certainty. In the Euthyphro, he demonstrates the method: ask for a definition, test it against cases, revise when the definition fails. The process is demanding, but it is not arbitrary.

This course follows Socrates' approach. It does not assume you already have all the right moral intuitions, and it does not simply ask you to report your feelings. It asks you to reason. That means stating your view precisely, considering objections, and being willing to revise when you cannot answer them. Ethics studied this way is genuinely demanding — which is what makes it genuinely rewarding.

Before you continue: In your notebook, write one sentence answering this: What does Euthyphro know about piety, and what does he lack? Then click to compare.
A strong answer includes both parts: Euthyphro knows what he intends to do and feels confident that it is pious. What he lacks is a definition — a principled account of what makes any action pious, which would allow him (or anyone) to evaluate cases correctly. He has moral certainty without moral justification.

💪 WHAT YOU HAVE LEARNED

Ethics begins not with doubt, but with honesty: the recognition that moral confidence and moral knowledge are not the same thing, and that closing the gap between them requires careful reasoning. Euthyphro left Socrates without an answer. You are at the beginning of the work he avoided. The rest of this course is that work — done rigorously, with the best thinkers who have taken it seriously.
