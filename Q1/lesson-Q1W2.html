📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Across every civilization we know of, people have tried to answer the question: what does morality require?
Before asking whether morality is objective, it is worth pausing to notice a remarkable fact: human beings everywhere and in every period have produced moral codes. This lesson examines what that universal moral impulse looks like across several ancient traditions, and asks what — if anything — the shared patterns among them suggest.

📚 PART 1 · FOUR VOICES FROM THE ANCIENT WORLD
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN · Before you read

Babylon: The Code of Hammurabi (c. 1754 BCE)

Carved onto a seven-foot stele of black diorite and discovered by French archaeologists in 1901, the Code of Hammurabi is one of the oldest surviving legal and moral codes in human history. King Hammurabi of Babylon opens it with a religious declaration: the gods Anu and Enlil have called him to make justice prevail in the land, to prevent the strong from oppressing the weak, to enlighten the land. The code contains 282 provisions covering commerce, property, family, wages, and punishment. What is philosophically significant is not just its age but its framing: Hammurabi presents law not as the king's arbitrary will, but as the gods' demand for order and fairness.

The code's famous principle of proportional punishment — an eye for an eye, a tooth for a tooth — is often caricatured as primitive cruelty. Its actual function was the opposite: it limited punishment to proportional response, preventing the unlimited retaliation that blood feuds otherwise permitted. The principle is an early attempt to embed the idea of fairness into law.

Israel: The Ten Commandments (c. 13th century BCE)

The Ten Commandments, recorded in Exodus 20, differ structurally from Hammurabi's code. They are not case law but absolute prohibitions and positive obligations, grounded in the covenant between God and the people of Israel. The first four commandments concern the relationship between the people and God. The remaining six are moral commands governing human relationships: honor your parents, do not kill, do not steal, do not bear false witness, do not covet what belongs to your neighbor.

What is philosophically notable here is the grounding: morality is not presented as the custom of the tribe or the preference of the king, but as the command of a God who is the source of all that exists. This gives moral demands an authority that transcends any human power. It also raises a question Plato would later frame precisely: do the gods command things because they are good, or are they good because the gods command them? That question — the Euthyphro dilemma — will return in Quarter 4 of this course.

China: The Analects of Confucius (5th century BCE)

The Analects record the teachings of Confucius (551–479 BCE), a contemporary of the early Socratic tradition. Confucius' ethical framework centers on ren, often translated as benevolence, humaneness, or goodness toward others. When a disciple asks Confucius if there is one word that can serve as the rule for one's entire life, he replies: "Is not shu — reciprocity — such a word? Do not impose on others what you yourself do not desire." This formulation of what we might call the negative Golden Rule appears independently across traditions separated by thousands of miles.

Confucius grounds ethics not in divine command but in the demands of proper human relationships: between ruler and subject, parent and child, husband and wife, elder brother and younger, friend and friend. The virtuous person fulfills the requirements of each relationship faithfully. This emphasis on character, relationship, and social role resonates strongly with the virtue ethics tradition you will study in Quarter 3.

Rome and Judea: The Sermon on the Mount (c. 30 CE)

The Sermon on the Mount (Matthew 5–7) is among the most influential moral documents in Western history. Jesus extends and deepens the commandment tradition: where the law prohibits murder, the Sermon prohibits even the anger that leads to it. Where the law prohibits adultery, the Sermon addresses the disposition of the heart. The famous positive formulation — "Do unto others as you would have them do unto you" — makes the obligation active, not merely restraining.

The Beatitudes that open the Sermon present a striking inversion of conventional moral accounting: the poor in spirit, the meek, the merciful are declared blessed. This is not a valorization of weakness, but an argument about where genuine human flourishing is actually found — a claim that will matter enormously when you encounter Aristotle's account of happiness in Quarter 3.

📚 PART 2 · WHAT THE CONVERGENCE MEANS

Convergence and Divergence

These four traditions differ in important ways. Hammurabi grounds law in divine delegation to a king. The Ten Commandments ground it in direct divine command. Confucius grounds it in the requirements of human relationships and proper social order. The Sermon on the Mount grounds it in a transformed understanding of human dignity and the demands of love. The metaphysical frameworks differ substantially.

Yet across these differences, certain moral demands recur with striking consistency:

This convergence is not something to be explained away. C.S. Lewis, writing in the mid-twentieth century, catalogued it extensively in the appendix to The Abolition of Man. His point was not that all moral codes are identical — they are not — but that certain moral demands appear across civilizations with a consistency that is difficult to explain if morality is merely a local custom or arbitrary preference. If morality were simply the habit of a tribe, we would expect moral codes to be as varied as culinary preferences. They are not.

What the Convergence Does and Does Not Prove

Cross-cultural moral convergence does not by itself prove that an objective moral law exists. It is possible — in principle — that the convergence is explained by shared evolutionary pressures, common social needs, or some other non-moral cause. These explanations will be examined in the Science and Ethics unit later this quarter.

What the convergence does show is this: the moral relativist's simplest claim — that different cultures have entirely different morals, so there is no common standard — is factually incorrect. The diversity is real, but so is the convergence. Any serious account of morality has to explain both.

💪 WHAT YOU HAVE LEARNED

Human beings everywhere have made moral judgments and constructed moral codes. The codes differ in their metaphysical foundations, but they converge on certain core demands with a consistency that is hard to explain if morality is simply the custom of a tribe. This convergence is neither proof of objective morality nor evidence against it. It is a fact that any serious theory of ethics must account for. The rest of this quarter will examine the leading theories — and their critics — with that fact in view.
