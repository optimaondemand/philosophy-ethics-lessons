Can Science Ground Ethics? Sentimentalism and Its Limits

📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
If reason cannot ground ethics, can feeling? And if feeling, which feelings — and whose?
Once it seems that moral claims cannot be derived from purely rational or empirical reasoning alone, a tempting alternative presents itself: ground ethics in human emotion. This approach — sentimentalism — was developed in the eighteenth century by Hume and Francis Hutcheson and remains influential today. This lesson examines what sentimentalism offers, where it genuinely succeeds, and where it runs into serious difficulty.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to define moral sentimentalism and explain how Hutcheson and Hume grounded ethics in natural human sympathy.
2
I will be able to explain what sentimentalism gets right about the role of emotion in moral life.
3
I will be able to explain why sentimentalism fails as a complete account of moral obligation, identifying the problems of moral conflict, correction, and the scope of sympathy.
📚 PART 1 · THE SENTIMENTAL TRADITION
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
When you see someone treated unfairly, do you feel something? What role do you think that feeling plays in your moral judgment — is it the source of the judgment, a confirmation of it, or something else?

Francis Hutcheson and the Moral Sense

Francis Hutcheson (1694–1746), a Scottish-Irish philosopher and the teacher of Adam Smith, proposed that human beings are equipped with a moral sense — an immediate faculty of approval or disapproval analogous to the physical senses. Just as we perceive colors without inference, Hutcheson argued, we perceive the moral quality of actions directly: benevolence appears immediately as good, cruelty as repugnant. This moral sense is part of human nature, given by God, and oriented toward the happiness of others. Ethics, on this view, is grounded in a natural human capacity, not in abstract reason.

Hume and Natural Sympathy

Hume developed a related position. While he is famous for the is/ought distinction examined in the previous lesson, Hume did not conclude that ethics was impossible. He grounded it instead in natural sympathy: the human capacity to feel, to some degree, what others feel. When we see another person in pain, we experience a reflected version of that pain. When we see someone flourish, we share in the pleasure. Moral judgments, on Hume's account, arise from this sympathetic response corrected by an impartial standpoint: we approve of actions that tend to benefit people in general, not just those we happen to like.

Sentimentalism in this tradition captures something genuinely important: human beings are not purely rational agents. Emotion is not an obstacle to moral judgment but part of its machinery. When we judge that something was cruel, we are not merely running a logical calculation; we react with something that resembles pain, and that reaction is not morally irrelevant. Aristotle himself insisted that virtue involves feeling as well as reasoning; a person who did the right thing without any emotional engagement would be missing something essential.

📚 PART 2 · WHERE SENTIMENTALISM RUNS OUT

Three Problems

Despite its genuine insight, sentimentalism faces three difficulties that it cannot resolve on its own terms.

1. The problem of sympathy's scope. Natural sympathy is not impartial. We feel more strongly for those close to us, those who resemble us, those we can see and hear. Hume was aware of this and introduced the corrective of an "impartial spectator" — imagining how a rational, disinterested observer would judge the situation. But once you introduce an impartial spectator, you have already moved beyond raw feeling. The impartial standpoint is a rational construction, not an emotion. Sentimentalism, to work as an ethical theory, must smuggle reason back in.

2. The problem of moral correction. We regularly judge that certain feelings are morally mistaken. A person who feels no sympathy for an outgroup is not simply having a different feeling; we judge that they are feeling wrongly, that their sympathy has been misdirected or corrupted. But if moral judgment just is feeling, what standard are we using to correct the feeling? The correction implies a standard that stands above any particular feeling — which is exactly what sentimentalism denies.

3. The problem of moral conflict. Emotions conflict. I feel sympathy for a friend who asks me to help him conceal wrongdoing; I also feel something like outrage at the wrongdoing itself. Sentimentalism has no resources to adjudicate between these competing feelings beyond appealing to some other feeling. But moral life regularly requires us to override one feeling on the basis of a principle — which is just what a purely sentiment-based ethics cannot provide.

Moral sentimentalism: The view that moral judgments are grounded in natural human emotions (sympathy, approval, disapproval), not in reason alone.

Impartial spectator: A theoretical device introduced by Hume and developed by Adam Smith: imagining how a rational, benevolent, and disinterested observer would evaluate an action. Used to correct the partiality of natural sympathy.

⚡ TRY IT · DOES SENTIMENTALISM HANDLE THIS?
For each scenario, decide: does moral sentimentalism provide a clear answer, or does the scenario expose a limit of the theory?
1. You see a young child fall and scrape their knee. You feel immediately moved to help. Sentimentalism says: approve actions that promote others' well-being.
2. You feel sympathy for a friend who committed a fraud and wants your help covering it up. You also feel sympathy for the people your friend defrauded. Both feelings are real. Which do you act on?
3. A philosopher argues that we have the same moral obligations to strangers in distant countries as to our own neighbors, because their suffering matters equally. Sentimentalism must explain this.

💪 WHAT YOU HAVE LEARNED

Sentimentalism is not wrong to insist that emotion is part of moral life. Aristotle agreed. But emotion alone cannot carry the full weight of moral obligation. The moment we need to adjudicate between competing feelings, correct a feeling that has gone astray, or extend moral consideration beyond the range of natural sympathy, we need something more — some standard that is not itself just another feeling. The search for that standard is the continuing project of this course.
