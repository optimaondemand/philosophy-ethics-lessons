Is Morality Just Our Custom? The Challenge of Moral Relativism
Lesson
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
If cultures disagree about what is right, does it follow that there is no right to disagree about?
Moral relativism — the view that moral truth is relative to cultures or individuals, with no universal standard above them — is one of the most widely held positions in contemporary life. It is also one that cannot withstand careful examination. This lesson examines relativism's appeal, its forms, and its fatal difficulties.

📚 PART 1 · RELATIVISM AND ITS APPEAL
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN

Protagoras and the Origin of Relativism

The oldest form of relativism in the Western tradition comes from the Sophist Protagoras (c. 490–420 BCE), a contemporary of Socrates. His most famous claim, preserved in Plato's Theaetetus, is this: "Man is the measure of all things — of things that are, that they are; of things that are not, that they are not." Applied to values, this means that what is good or right is determined by each individual's or community's judgment. There is no standard above human opinion to which human opinion is answerable.

Contemporary moral relativism comes in two main forms. Cultural relativism holds that moral truth is relative to the standards of a given culture or society: what is right in one culture may be wrong in another, and neither culture's standard is objectively superior. Individual relativism (sometimes called subjectivism) holds that moral truth is relative to the judgment of each individual person. Both forms share the denial of any universal moral standard.

The Argument from Disagreement

The most common argument for moral relativism runs as follows:

Premise 1: If morality were objective, rational inquiry would eventually produce convergence, as it does in science.

Premise 2: There is persistent, deep moral disagreement.

Conclusion: Morality is not objective; it is relative to culture or individual.

Notice, first, that this argument is logically valid. If rational inquiry into an objective domain always converges, and moral inquiry has not converged, then morality is not an objective domain; that is a straightforward denial of the consequent, the same form as "if it rained, the street would be wet; the street is not wet; so it did not rain." The relativist cannot be answered here by pointing to a bad inference, the way an argument that simply moved from "people disagree" to "there is no fact of the matter" could be. The weakness has to be found in a premise, and Premise 1 is where it lies.

Premise 1 assumes that any domain of objective truth must produce rational convergence, on the model of science. But science converges for reasons that are specific to science: controlled experiment, repeatable observation, and a settled method for adjudicating between rival explanations. Moral inquiry has never had those tools, whether or not moral truths exist. A field's failure to converge is a contingent fact about how that field investigates its subject, not a precondition every objective domain must meet before it counts as objective. Historians still argue about the causes of the First World War; mathematicians disputed the foundations of their own discipline for decades. Slow or absent convergence shows that a question is hard to settle, not that there is nothing to settle.

Moreover, as we saw in the previous lesson, the extent of moral disagreement is itself exaggerated. Ancient moral codes from different cultures converge substantially on certain core demands. Much apparent moral disagreement reflects disagreement about facts (whether a given action has certain consequences) or about metaphysics (whether the souls of the dead need to be appeased), not disagreement about the underlying moral principles. Premise 2 is weaker than it first appears.

📚 PART 2 · WHY RELATIVISM FAILS

The Self-Refutation Problem

The most powerful objection to relativism is that it is self-undermining. Consider what the relativist is actually asserting: "Moral claims are only true relative to the standards of the person or culture making them; there is no universal moral standard." But this statement itself presents a universal claim about the nature of morality. It does not say "relative to my culture, morality is relative." It says morality as such has no universal standard. That is a claim the relativist intends to be true for everyone — which is precisely what the relativist's own position forbids.

Plato puts this point with characteristic precision in the Theaetetus. If Protagoras is right that each person's judgment determines truth for that person, then the judgment of those who believe Protagoras is wrong is, by Protagoras' own standard, true for them. Protagoras' position refutes itself the moment it is stated.

The Tolerance Objection

Relativism is often presented as a foundation for tolerance: if no culture's values are objectively superior to another's, then no one should judge or interfere with other cultures' practices. But this reasoning is incoherent on relativist grounds. If there is no universal moral standard, then there is no universal standard that says tolerance is right. The relativist who urges tolerance is making a universal moral claim — that all cultures have an obligation to tolerate each other — which contradicts the relativist's premise.

Furthermore, cultural relativism taken seriously implies that we cannot morally criticize any cultural practice on any grounds external to that culture. Historical practices that virtually everyone now judges to have been deeply unjust — the legal ownership of persons, the systematic exclusion of women from public life — would, on strict cultural relativism, be immune from moral criticism, because they were sanctioned by the cultures that practiced them. This implication is one most relativists are not willing to accept — but if they resist it, they are implicitly appealing to a standard that transcends the cultural consensus.

The Practical Objection: Moral Progress

Relativism cannot account for the concept of moral progress. If what is right is just what a culture approves of, then the abolition of chattel slavery in the nineteenth century was not moral progress — it was simply a change in cultural preference, no more significant morally than a change in fashion. But almost no one believes this. The very language of moral progress — that we have come to understand something better than our ancestors did — presupposes a standard of moral truth by which we can judge past and present practices.

Moral relativism: The view that moral claims are true only relative to a culture or individual, with no universal moral standard.

Cultural relativism: The form of relativism that grounds moral truth in cultural standards.

Individual relativism (subjectivism): The form of relativism that grounds moral truth in individual judgment.

Self-refutation: When a position's own standards undermine or contradict the position itself.

💪 WHAT YOU HAVE LEARNED

Moral relativism is not a sophisticated position. It is an intuitively appealing reaction to the complexity of moral disagreement, but it collapses under examination. The argument from disagreement is logically invalid. The appeal to tolerance is self-contradicting. The implied denial of moral progress defies what virtually everyone believes when they think carefully about historical change. This does not mean moral questions are easy; it means that the relativist shortcut is not available. We are stuck with the hard work of reasoning through them.
