Is Morality Just Feeling? The Challenge of Emotivism

📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
When you say "cruelty is wrong," are you stating a fact — or just expressing how cruelty makes you feel?
One of the most influential obstacles to serious ethical reasoning is the view that moral language does not actually say anything true or false. On this view, moral claims are disguised emotional reactions, not genuine statements. This view is called emotivism. Understanding it clearly — and seeing why it fails — is essential preparation for everything that follows in this course.

📚 PART 1 · THE CASE FOR EMOTIVISM
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN

Vienna and the Age of Science

In the 1920s, a group of philosophers working in Vienna developed a theory called logical positivism: the view that only two kinds of statements are genuinely meaningful. The first kind are statements that can be verified by empirical observation — scientific claims. The second are statements that are true by definition — mathematical and logical claims. Everything else, including metaphysics, theology, and moral claims, was held to be literally meaningless: not false, but devoid of cognitive content.

A.J. Ayer, a British philosopher, brought this view to a wide audience in his 1936 book Language, Truth and Logic. When Ayer turned to moral claims, he argued that sentences like "stealing is wrong" do not assert anything that could be true or false. Instead, they express the speaker's attitude toward stealing — something equivalent to adding an exclamation of disapproval. To say "stealing is wrong" is, in Ayer's analysis, no more cognitively meaningful than saying "stealing — ugh!"

This view became known as emotivism: the theory that moral utterances are expressions of emotion, not statements of fact. The philosopher Charles Stevenson developed it further, arguing that moral language functions primarily to influence others' attitudes and behavior, not to describe the world.

Why Emotivism Was Appealing

Emotivism had real intellectual appeal in an age dominated by scientific thinking. If the only reliable way of knowing is empirical observation, then moral claims — which cannot be weighed, measured, or observed in a laboratory — seem to have no claim to truth. The frequency of moral disagreement also seemed to support the view: if morality were objective, we might expect agreement to converge over time, the way agreement about scientific facts converges. Instead, moral disputes seem intractable.

Many people hold a version of emotivism today without knowing it has a name. When someone says "that's just your opinion" in response to a moral argument, or when a political debate ends with "we just have different values," the underlying assumption is often emotivist: moral claims express personal or cultural attitudes, not objective truths.

📚 PART 2 · WHY EMOTIVISM FAILS

The Problem of Moral Argument

If emotivism is true, what exactly is happening when two people argue about a moral question? If "stealing is wrong" just means "stealing — boo!", then a disagreement about theft is just a clash of feelings. There is nothing to resolve by reasoning. You cannot refute someone's feeling of disgust any more than you can argue with a sneeze.

But this is plainly not how moral argument works. When people argue about whether an action was unjust, they give reasons. They appeal to principles. They point out facts about consequences, intentions, and relationships. They accuse each other of inconsistency. None of this makes sense if moral claims are just emotional noise. The very practice of moral argument — which human beings engage in constantly — presupposes that there is something to reason about.

The Frege-Geach Problem

The philosopher Peter Geach identified a technical but decisive problem with emotivism. Consider this argument:

Premise 1: If stealing is wrong, then getting your little brother to steal is wrong.

Premise 2: Stealing is wrong.

Conclusion: Getting your little brother to steal is wrong.

This is a valid argument. But if "stealing is wrong" in Premise 2 is just an expression of disgust, then "stealing is wrong" in Premise 1 is also just an expression of disgust — not the assertion of a fact that the conditional depends on. On emotivist grounds, the word "wrong" cannot mean the same thing in both premises. The whole structure of moral reasoning, which depends on treating moral predicates as stable parts of sentences that can be combined in logical inference, collapses.

The Stability of Moral Perception

A third objection concerns what we might call the stability of moral perception. When we say that gratuitous cruelty is wrong, we do not mean merely that we dislike it. We mean that it is wrong even for those who like it, even in cultures that approve of it, even if no one is around to feel disgusted by it. Moral claims have a characteristic universality and objectivity that mere expressions of feeling do not. If I say "broccoli is disgusting," I do not mean that broccoli is disgusting for everyone. If I say "torturing children for entertainment is wrong," I mean exactly that — for everyone, everywhere. Emotivism cannot account for this difference.

Emotivism: The view that moral utterances express feelings or attitudes rather than asserting truths. Associated with A.J. Ayer and Charles Stevenson.

Logical positivism: The philosophical movement holding that only empirically verifiable or analytically true statements are meaningful. Led to emotivism when applied to moral language.

The Frege-Geach problem: The objection that emotivism cannot account for the logical role of moral predicates in valid arguments, because it treats moral terms as expressions rather than stable assertions.

💪 WHAT YOU HAVE LEARNED

Emotivism is a serious philosophical position, not a straw man. It captured something real: moral claims are connected to feelings, and the connection between moral language and empirical verification is not obvious. But when pressed, emotivism cannot account for the most basic features of moral life — the fact that we reason about moral questions, that moral claims seem to apply universally, and that moral predicates function like ordinary predicates in logical arguments. The view that morality is "just feeling" turns out, on examination, to be harder to defend than it appears.
