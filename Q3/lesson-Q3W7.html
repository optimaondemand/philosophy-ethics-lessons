Friendship and the Good Life: Aristotle on Philia
Lesson
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Is friendship a luxury for a good life, or a necessity?
Aristotle devotes two of the ten books of the Nicomachean Ethics (Books VIII and IX) to friendship. This is a striking proportion: nearly one-fifth of his entire ethical treatise. Most contemporary ethical theories treat friendship as a morally neutral personal matter, or even as a potential source of bias. Aristotle takes the opposite view: genuine friendship is not only a component of the good life but something no human being can flourish without. This lesson examines Aristotle's three-tier account of friendship, explains why the highest kind is so rare and so valuable, and shows how his account of friendship follows directly from his account of virtue and human nature.

I will be able to distinguish Aristotle's three types of friendship and explain the basis of each.
2

3

📚 PART 1 · THREE KINDS OF FRIENDSHIP
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
Think of three people you consider friends. Do you like all three for the same reasons? What is the basis of each relationship?

Philia: More Than What We Mean by "Friendship"

The Greek word philia covers a wider territory than the English word "friendship." It includes close friendships, but also ties of affection between parents and children, between business partners, and between citizens. The root meaning is love with mutual goodwill: both parties wish well to the other and both know it. When that mutual knowledge is absent, you may have goodwill toward someone, but not friendship.

Aristotle argues that all friendships are based on one of three things: utility, pleasure, or virtue. This gives him his three-tier taxonomy.

ARISTOTLE'S THREE-TIER TAXONOMY OF FRIENDSHIP (NE VIII)
Utility Friendship
Based on: mutual usefulness. Each person values the other for the benefits received.
Example: two business partners; a student and a classmate who share notes.
Stability: Low. The friendship dissolves when the utility ends.
Pleasure Friendship
Based on: the enjoyment each person takes in the other. Each values the other for the pleasure the relationship provides.
Example: friends who enjoy the same activity; acquaintances who are witty and entertaining together.
Stability: Moderate. Dissolves if tastes change or life circumstances separate the parties.
Virtue Friendship
Based on: mutual recognition of and respect for each other's good character. Each loves the other for who they are, not for what they provide.
Example: two people who share deep values, help each other become better, and wish for each other's genuine good.
Stability: High. Lasts as long as both remain good, because what is loved does not change.

Aristotle does not say utility and pleasure friendships are false or bad. They are genuine friendships with genuine value. But they are, as he says, friendships only "incidentally" — the friend is valued for something external, not for himself.

📚 PART 2 · WHY VIRTUE-FRIENDSHIP IS NECESSARY

The Argument from Human Nature

Aristotle says in Book IX that "a human being is a social and political animal" and that living a solitary life is not merely lonely but a failure to live a properly human life. Virtue-friendship is not an ornament added to a life already complete; it is partly constitutive of the good life. Why?

Aristotle's argument runs as follows. Virtuous activity is the highest good. But it is very difficult to exercise virtues fully in isolation: courage, justice, and generosity all require a community of persons toward whom you act. Moreover, knowing yourself is difficult: we cannot see ourselves clearly, but we can see our friends clearly, and they can see us. A virtue-friend who knows your character and tells you the truth about it provides something no amount of self-examination can supply. Finally, virtuous friends help each other continue to be virtuous: "living together" with good people shapes character in the way that habituation shapes it initially. The philosopher who lives among scholars becomes more learned; the generous person surrounded by generous friends becomes more reliably generous.

The Right Kind of Self-Love

Aristotle makes a striking distinction in Book IX between two senses of self-love. The person who is a "self-lover" in the bad sense assigns the most goods to herself: wealth, honor, and bodily pleasures. This is what we ordinarily mean by selfishness. But Aristotle says there is a different, correct sense of self-love: assigning to yourself the most excellent activities, above all virtuous action and intellectual inquiry. The person who loves herself in this correct sense will also be a better friend, because she has more genuinely good things to share and contribute.

The connection between self-love and friendship becomes clear when Aristotle says: "the virtuous man's relation to his friend is the same as his relation to himself, for his friend is another self." The good friend is not someone who sacrifices herself entirely for another, nor someone who uses another entirely for herself: she is someone who recognizes her friend's genuine good as genuinely good.

Think about this first, then open: How would Aristotle respond to the objection that virtue-friendship leads to nepotism or favoritism? Write or think through your answer before clicking.
Aristotle's response: The objection confuses two domains. Aristotle acknowledges that in matters of justice, impartiality is required: you cannot assign goods unfairly just because someone is your friend. But friendship is not a domain of justice; it is a domain of love and mutual goodwill. It is entirely appropriate to give more attention, time, and care to your virtue-friend than to strangers. The just person treats acquaintances and strangers fairly; she is not obligated to love them equally to her closest friends. What virtue-friendship cannot do is exempt a friend from the demands of justice: if your friend is corrupt, loyalty does not require you to help him conceal it.

💪 WHAT YOU HAVE LEARNED

Aristotle distinguishes three kinds of friendship based on utility, pleasure, and virtue. Only virtue-friendship involves loving the other person for who they are: it is grounded in mutual recognition of good character and involves genuine care for each other's flourishing. Aristotle regards this kind of friendship as necessary, not optional, for the good life: it enables virtuous activity, self-knowledge, and the continued formation of character that eudaimonia requires.
