The Golden Mean: Virtue as Excellence Between Extremes

📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Virtue is a mean between two vices: the vice of excess and the vice of deficiency.
Aristotle's doctrine of the mean (mesotes) is one of the most recognized ideas in all of philosophy. But it is frequently misunderstood. The mean is not a mathematical average or a recipe for mediocrity. It is a claim about what excellence looks like in any domain: excellence always involves the right amount, in the right way, toward the right people, at the right time. This lesson examines the doctrine carefully, applies it to specific virtues, and addresses the main objections to it.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to explain Aristotle's doctrine of the mean and distinguish it from the misunderstanding that virtue is "moderation in all things."
2
I will be able to apply the mean structure to at least three specific virtues and identify their corresponding vices of excess and deficiency.
3
I will be able to explain why the mean is "relative to us" and what that means for moral guidance.
📚 PART 1 · THE DOCTRINE OF THE MEAN
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
Is courage the same as fearlessness? Is a soldier who feels no fear at all during combat the most courageous soldier?

The Structure of the Mean

In Book II, Chapter 6, Aristotle defines virtue of character as "a state of character concerned with choice, lying in a mean, i.e., the mean relative to us, this being determined by a rational principle, and by that principle by which the man of practical wisdom would determine it." This dense definition repays careful unpacking.

Every human response — emotion, action, or both — admits of too much and too little. Both excess and deficiency are forms of failure; both are vices. The virtue is the appropriate amount: the response that hits the mark given the specific circumstances, the specific persons involved, the specific purpose, and the specific time. This is the "mean relative to us": not the mathematical midpoint, but what is right for this person in this situation.

The mean is determined by practical reason, specifically by what a person of practical wisdom (phronesis) would determine. This prevents the doctrine from collapsing into relativism: the mean is not "whatever seems right to any individual" but "what someone with good character and good judgment would recognize as appropriate."

THE STRUCTURE OF VIRTUE: COURAGE AS EXAMPLE
VICE OF DEFICIENCY
Cowardice
Excessive fear; deficient confidence
VIRTUE / THE MEAN
Courage
Right fear and confidence, at the right time
VICE OF EXCESS
Recklessness
Deficient fear; excessive confidence
📚 PART 2 · THE VIRTUES IN THE MEAN STRUCTURE

Aristotle applies the mean structure to a wide range of virtues and the domains of action and emotion they govern. The table below shows the pattern across several key virtues:

Domain Vice of Deficiency Virtue (the Mean) Vice of Excess
Fear / danger Cowardice Courage Recklessness
Bodily pleasures Insensibility Temperance Self-indulgence
Giving money Miserliness Generosity Prodigality
Self-presentation Understatement Truthfulness Boastfulness
Anger Spiritlessness Good temper Irascibility

One feature worth noticing: Aristotle explicitly includes the vice of deficiency as a vice. The person who never feels anger at genuine injustice, who smiles blandly at genuine wrongdoing, lacks something morally important. Spiritlessness is not a virtue; it is the failure to have the appropriate emotional response. Genuine good character requires that the emotions function well, not that they be suppressed.

📚 PART 3 · OBJECTIONS AND CLARIFICATIONS

Three Common Misunderstandings

Misunderstanding 1: "The mean" means "mediocre." The mean is not a lukewarm or middling response. Aristotle says explicitly: virtue is an extreme with respect to excellence, even though it is a mean with respect to the two vices. The courageous response to a dangerous situation might require extraordinary action; it is "mean" only in the sense that it avoids both the excess of recklessness and the deficiency of cowardice.

Misunderstanding 2: "The mean" is the same for everyone. Aristotle says it is "relative to us." What counts as courage for a professional soldier differs from what counts as courage for a civilian. The appropriate response to a difficult conversation depends on who you are, who the other person is, what the stakes are, and what the relationship is. There is no single arithmetic midpoint that applies equally to all.

Misunderstanding 3: The mean structure applies to all moral considerations. Aristotle is clear: some actions and emotions are simply wrong and have no virtuous mean. There is no "right amount" of murder, of adultery, of malicious spite. These are wrong as such; their very names imply excess. This is Aristotle's agreement with deontology on one point: some actions are simply prohibited, and no situational calibration can make them acceptable.

💪 WHAT YOU HAVE LEARNED

Virtue is a mean between two vices: the vice of excess and the vice of deficiency. The mean is not a mechanical average but the response that is appropriate to the specific situation, determined by practical wisdom. It is "relative to us" in the sense that what counts as the right response depends on the person, the circumstances, and the relationship, not on a single standard applied uniformly. And the mean structure applies to actions and emotions together: the virtuous person feels the right things and acts rightly, not just one or the other.
