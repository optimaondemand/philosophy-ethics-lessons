The Cardinal Virtues: Courage, Temperance, Justice, and Prudence
Lesson
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
What are the virtues that every human being needs in order to live well?
The four cardinal virtues — courage, temperance, justice, and prudence (practical wisdom) — form the backbone of the classical ethical tradition. They appear in Plato's Republic, are developed in Aristotle's Nicomachean Ethics, and were systematized in the medieval tradition by Thomas Aquinas (1225–1274), who integrated them with the Christian theological virtues of faith, hope, and charity. They remain the core of the Western virtue tradition. This lesson examines each cardinal virtue in depth, with attention to why each one is necessary for human flourishing and how each can be malformed.

Cardinal: From the Latin Cardo, a Hinge

The word "cardinal" comes from the Latin cardo, meaning hinge: these virtues are the hinges on which the moral life turns. They appear first in Plato's Republic (each virtue governing a part of the soul and a part of the city), are developed in Aristotle's Nicomachean Ethics, and are given their canonical form in Aquinas's Summa Theologica. They are called cardinal not because they are arbitrary cultural choices but because each one addresses a fundamental dimension of human action and character that no person can flourish without.

📚 PART 2 · THE FOUR CARDINAL VIRTUES
Courage (Andreia)
The mean between cowardice and recklessness in the domain of fear and danger. The courageous person faces genuinely fearful things in the right way, at the right time, for the right reasons. Aristotle regards this as the foundational virtue: without it, you cannot act on any other virtue when doing so involves difficulty or risk. The coward knows what is right but cannot bring himself to do it.

Temperance (Sophrosyne)
The mean between insensibility and self-indulgence in the domain of bodily pleasures. The temperate person is not enslaved to appetite: she takes pleasure in the right things in the right amounts. Aristotle is not an ascetic: he does not say pleasures are bad, but that they must be ordered rather than dominant. The intemperate person allows appetite to override reason and gradually loses the capacity for self-governance.

Justice (Dikaiosyne)
Aristotle calls justice "the greatest of the virtues" and "a complete virtue" because it is the only virtue directed entirely toward others. The just person gives each person their due: neither more nor less than what they are owed. There are two kinds: distributive justice (fair distribution of goods and burdens) and corrective justice (rectifying wrongs). Justice is not just a disposition but a relationship: it requires a community of persons between whom claims and obligations exist.
Tap to expand
Prudence (Phronesis)
Practical wisdom, or the capacity to deliberate well about what conduces to living well overall. Aristotle makes this the master intellectual virtue governing all the moral virtues. Without phronesis, the moral virtues become rigid traits that misfire in unusual situations. With it, a person perceives what each situation calls for and responds accordingly. This is why Aquinas (following Aristotle) places prudence among the cardinal virtues: it is not merely a technique but a form of moral excellence.

Justice as a Special Case

Aristotle devotes the entire Book V of the Nicomachean Ethics to justice, more than any other virtue. He says justice is the complete virtue in relation to others: if you are just, you exercise virtue in all your dealings with other people, not merely with yourself. "The just man," Aristotle says, "is the man who exercises his good qualities in his relations with other people; whereas the unjust man uses others for his own ends."

This differs importantly from the Kantian rights-based account. For Kant, justice is primarily a matter of non-violation: you are just when you do not violate others' rights. For Aristotle, justice is a positive virtue: it involves actively recognizing what others are owed and giving it to them. The just person is not merely someone who refrains from stealing; she is someone who actively attends to what fairness requires in her dealings with others.

💪 WHAT YOU HAVE LEARNED

The four cardinal virtues — courage, temperance, justice, and prudence — are the virtues on which the moral life hinges. Each addresses a fundamental dimension of human action and character: how we face danger and difficulty; how we order our appetite and pleasure; how we treat others and give them their due; and how we perceive and deliberate about what each situation calls for. Together they constitute the framework of human excellence that makes flourishing possible.
