📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
If every kind of thing has a characteristic function, what is the characteristic function of a human being?
Aristotle's claim that eudaimonia is the highest good needs grounding. Why should flourishing count as the highest good rather than pleasure or anything else? Aristotle's answer lies in his account of human nature: every natural thing has a telos — a characteristic end or purpose that defines what kind of thing it is and what it means for it to be excellent. The human telos grounds the definition of eudaimonia and connects ethics to a broader picture of what human beings are. This lesson examines that argument and its implications.

📚 PART 1 · TELOS AND FUNCTION

Telos: Purpose and Completion

Telos is the Greek word for end, purpose, or completion. It is the root of our English word "teleology" (study of purposes or ends). For Aristotle, understanding what a thing is requires understanding what it is for, what it is trying to become, what its completed form is. An acorn's telos is to become an oak tree. A doctor's telos is health for her patients. A knife's telos is to cut well. Each kind of thing has a characteristic activity or function, and excellence in that kind of thing consists in performing that function well.

This teleological framework is characteristically Aristotelian — his teacher Plato shared something of it, but Aristotle developed it with far greater systematic precision. For Aristotle, the natural world is organized by purposes: things have natures that incline them toward specific ends, and understanding those natures is the key to understanding what they are and what would count as their excellence or defect.

📚 PART 2 · THE FUNCTION ARGUMENT

Aristotle's Argument in NE I.7

In Book I, Chapter 7, Aristotle offers the most important argument of the Nicomachean Ethics. It runs as follows: just as a flute player, a sculptor, and a craftsman each have a characteristic function, a human being as such must also have a characteristic function. What is that function? It cannot be mere life, because plants have life. It cannot be sentient life, because animals share that with us. What is distinctive of human beings — and therefore constitutive of what it means to be a human being at all — is the capacity for rational activity. The human function, therefore, is the active exercise of the soul's faculties in accordance with reason, or at least not without reason.

THE FUNCTION ARGUMENT RECONSTRUCTED
P1. Every kind of thing that has a function has an excellence that consists in performing that function well.
P2. Human beings have a characteristic function: the active exercise of the soul in accordance with reason.
P3. Human excellence (virtue) consists in performing this function well, that is, in accordance with excellence (virtue).
C. Therefore: eudaimonia is the active exercise of the soul's faculties in accordance with virtue (excellence), over a complete life.

Several features of this conclusion deserve careful attention. First: it says "over a complete life." This is why Aristotle quotes with approval Solon's saying that we should "call no man happy until he is dead." Flourishing is not a single moment of well-being; it is what a whole life, taken together, amounts to. Second: it identifies flourishing with excellent rational activity, not with pleasant feelings. Feeling good is a consequence and accompaniment of flourishing, not its constituent. Third: the inclusion of "in accordance with virtue" makes ethics central to flourishing, not incidental to it.

📚 PART 3 · OBJECTIONS TO THE FUNCTION ARGUMENT

Does Human Nature Ground Ethics?

The function argument has been criticized on several grounds. The most influential objection: Hume's is/ought gap (Lesson 1.05 of this course). Even if human beings have a natural function, why should fulfilling it be what we morally ought to do? Deriving an "ought" from an "is" about human nature looks like the naturalistic fallacy. A human being's nature includes the capacity for cruelty as well as for reason. Why privilege reason as the norm?

Aristotle's response: the function argument is not trying to derive moral obligations from natural facts in the way the is/ought problem identifies. It is asking what genuine human excellence is, given what kind of thing a human being is. The response to "cruelty is also natural" is: yes, and a knife can also be used to pry open cans, but that is not what defines its excellence. The function identifies the norm internal to a kind, not an arbitrary selection from a range of possibilities. The excellences that constitute human flourishing are those in which the distinctively human capacities are fully and well exercised.

A second objection: does the argument assume that human beings have a single function, and is that assumption warranted? Aristotle's defenders note that his claim is modest: not that every human being has the same specific occupation, but that the capacity for rational activity is constitutive of being human at all — which is much harder to deny.

💪 WHAT YOU HAVE LEARNED

The function argument grounds Aristotle's ethics in an account of human nature. Every kind of thing has a characteristic function that defines what it is and what its excellence consists in. The characteristic function of human beings is the active exercise of the soul's faculties in accordance with reason; therefore human excellence consists in performing this activity in accordance with virtue.
