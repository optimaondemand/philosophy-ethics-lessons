Weakness of Will: When We Know What Is Right and Do Otherwise
Lesson
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Why do we so often know what is right and still fail to do it?
One of the oldest puzzles in ethics is the phenomenon the Greeks called akrasia: weakness of will, or acting contrary to one's better judgment. You know you should exercise; you stay on the couch. You know the cruel remark will wound; you make it anyway. Socrates argued that this is impossible: if you know what is good, you will necessarily pursue it. Aristotle disagrees. He takes akrasia seriously as a real phenomenon and devotes Book VII of the Nicomachean Ethics to explaining how it is possible, what distinguishes it from mere self-indulgence, and why it matters so deeply for virtue ethics.

📚 PART 1 · THE SOCRATIC PUZZLE
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
Have you ever done something you knew was wrong at the moment you did it? Not something you regretted afterward, but something you judged to be wrong as you were doing it?

Socrates: Weakness of Will Is Impossible

In Plato's Protagoras, Socrates argues that no one does wrong voluntarily. His argument is elegant: if you know that action A is genuinely better than action B, you will choose A. If you choose B, that shows you did not really know A was better. What appears to be weakness of will is actually ignorance: the person who eats the cake when she knows she should not has really, in that moment, succumbed to the belief that the pleasure now outweighs the benefit later. The knowledge slipped away under the pressure of appetite. For Socrates, virtue is a form of knowledge, and vice is always a form of ignorance.

Aristotle's Disagreement

Aristotle opens Book VII by saying that the Socratic position "seems to conflict with the observed facts." He takes seriously the phenomenology of akrasia: people sometimes act contrary to knowledge they actually have, not merely contrary to knowledge they have lost. He distinguishes two kinds of practical knowledge: knowing a general principle ("overeating is harmful") and applying it to the particular case at hand ("this second helping is an instance of overeating"). The person in the grip of appetite may still hold the general principle but fail to bring it to bear on the particular case. The knowledge is present but, as Aristotle says, "not operative": like a person who knows the theorems of geometry but cannot apply them when drunk.

ARISTOTLE'S CHARACTER TAXONOMY (NE VII)
Type Relation to Desire Relation to Judgment Aristotle's Evaluation
Virtuous Desires the right things in the right amounts; desire and reason aligned. Acts in accord with correct judgment, which is also his desire. Best character: does right without inner struggle.
Continent Has bad or disordered desires, but does not follow them. Acts in accord with correct judgment, despite inner struggle. Good but not fully virtuous: does right by effort, not by second nature.
Incontinent (Akratic) Has bad or disordered desires and follows them, despite knowing better. Knows what is right; acts against this knowledge under the pull of appetite. Worse than continent. Better than vicious: still capable of remorse and reform.
Vicious Desires bad things; has fully disordered appetite. Does wrong voluntarily and without inner conflict; judges bad things to be good. Worst character: not capable of remorse; does not see his actions as wrong.

The critical distinction: the akratic person retains the ability to judge his actions as wrong and to feel shame and regret. The vicious person does not. This is why the akratic person is curable and the vicious person is very difficult to reform.

📚 PART 2 · AKRASIA AND VIRTUE ETHICS

Why Akrasia Matters for Virtue Ethics

Akrasia is a more serious challenge for virtue ethics than for consequentialism or deontology. For those theories, ethics is primarily a matter of what you know and what you decide: as long as you act rightly, your inner states and desires are secondary. For Aristotle, virtue requires that desire and reason be harmonized through habituation. Akrasia is evidence that this harmony has not been achieved: the person knows what is right in some theoretical sense, but her character has not been formed so that she reliably acts on that knowledge.

This is why Aristotle says virtue ethics is not primarily a doctrine you learn but a form of training you undergo. A person who has been properly habituated will find, over time, that her desires align with her knowledge: she will not merely know that temperance is correct but will take genuine pleasure in temperate activity. The continent person who resists bad desires by force of will is in a more precarious position than the virtuous person in whom the conflict does not arise.

Think about this first, then open: Is the akratic person morally responsible for her actions? Write or think through your answer before opening.
Aristotle's position: Yes, the akratic person is morally responsible. She acted voluntarily and against her own judgment: she was not compelled, not ignorant of what was right, and not acting on a reasonable mistake. The fact that appetite was powerful does not excuse her in the way that genuine ignorance or compulsion would. However, Aristotle draws a distinction between straightforward akrasia (acting contrary to correct judgment under the immediate pressure of appetite) and the person who through repeated choices has formed a character predisposed to weakness. In the latter case, the person is also responsible for the character she has built, just as someone who injures themselves through negligent behavior is responsible for the injury even though she is not the direct cause of the disability.

Remorse, Shame, and Moral Recovery

One of Aristotle's most important observations is that the akratic person remains capable of genuine remorse. After acting against her judgment, she recognizes her failure as a failure. This is not a minor point. It means the akratic person retains a working moral compass; the problem is not that she cannot tell right from wrong but that her character has not been formed strongly enough to act reliably on that knowledge. For Aristotle, this is what makes moral improvement possible. The vicious person, who has corrupted his judgment and no longer recognizes his actions as wrong, is far harder to help.

💪 WHAT YOU HAVE LEARNED

Weakness of will, or akrasia, is the phenomenon of acting against one's better judgment. Socrates denied this was possible; Aristotle treats it as real and philosophically important. His analysis distinguishes the virtuous person (whose desires are aligned with reason), the continent person (who resists bad desires by force of will), the incontinent person (who acts against correct judgment under the pull of appetite), and the vicious person (whose judgment has been corrupted and who no longer judges his actions as wrong). The akratic person retains her moral compass and her capacity for remorse; this is what makes moral improvement possible.
