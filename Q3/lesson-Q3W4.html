📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
We become just by doing just things; we become courageous by doing courageous things.
This sentence from Book II of the Nicomachean Ethics is one of the most important in the history of philosophy. It encapsulates Aristotle's account of moral formation: virtues are not given to us at birth, nor are they implanted by divine command. They are acquired through practice, in the same way that skills are acquired. This insight has profound implications for how we understand moral education, moral failure, and the relationship between individual choice and communal life.

📚 PART 1 · HOW CHARACTER IS FORMED
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN

Virtues Are Not Natural, But Not Unnatural Either

Aristotle opens Book II by distinguishing moral virtues (ethike arete) from intellectual virtues (dianoetike arete). Intellectual virtues like theoretical wisdom are developed mainly by teaching and instruction. Moral virtues are different: they arise through ethos — habit, custom, repeated practice. (Notice: the word ethics comes from ethos; moral character is, at its etymological root, the product of custom and habit.) This is why virtues are neither natural nor contrary to nature: we are by nature capable of receiving the virtues, and habit brings them to completion.

Aristotle's analogy: we learn by doing. We become builders by building and musicians by playing music. Similarly, we become courageous by doing courageous acts, just by doing just acts, and temperate by doing temperate acts. This is not circular: the early acts are performed under guidance, perhaps reluctantly, without the full virtue. Through repetition, the character is gradually formed. What began as an action performed with difficulty becomes second nature — not because it has become effortless in the trivial sense, but because it has become the agent's own settled way of responding.

📚 PART 2 · VIRTUE AS STABLE DISPOSITION

The Definition of Virtue

Aristotle defines virtue as a stable disposition (hexis) of character that reliably produces responses in accordance with right reason. A disposition is different from both a capacity (which you may have without exercising it) and a passion (which happens to you): it is a settled tendency to respond in a certain way, developed through practice, that has become characteristic of the person.

Critically, full virtue requires not just acting in accordance with the mean, but acting with the right emotions. The person who does the right thing but feels the wrong emotions — who gives generously but does so resentfully, or who refrains from injustice but wishes he could be unjust — does not have the full virtue. Aristotle says: "The man who does not rejoice in noble actions is not even good; since no one would call a man just who did not enjoy acting justly, nor any man liberal who did not enjoy liberal actions" (NE I.8). The emotion is part of the virtue, not merely a pleasant accompaniment to it.

This is one of the deepest differences between Aristotle and Kant. For Kant, acting from inclination or emotion has no moral worth; only acting from duty does. For Aristotle, if you have genuinely acquired a virtue, you will take pleasure in acting virtuously: the emotion and the action are aligned. A person who acts justly while despising justice has not yet acquired the virtue of justice.

📚 PART 3 · MALFORMATION OF CHARACTER

What Happens When Habituation Goes Wrong

If virtues are acquired through habituation, then vices can also be acquired through habituation. A person who habitually acts cowardly — avoiding difficult situations, running from challenges, making excuses — gradually becomes cowardly. A person who habitually indulges every appetite without restraint becomes intemperate. The process works in both directions: the same mechanism that builds virtue also builds vice.

This raises a question about moral responsibility: is a person who was badly formed by their upbringing responsible for the vices they have? Aristotle is clear: we are responsible for our characters because we were responsible for the actions from which our characters were formed, even if the early actions were performed without a fully formed character to guide them. The origin of the character lies in choices, and those choices, however early and however influenced by environment, are genuine choices. "We are what we repeatedly do," as a later paraphrase of Aristotle expresses it.

Think through this moral responsibility question in your notebook, then read the analysis below.

The objection: if character is formed largely in childhood, and children have limited capacity for genuine choice, how can adults be responsible for characters they did not choose?

Aristotle's response has two parts. First, even children make genuine choices, and those choices gradually shape character. Second, the adult who has a badly formed character still has some capacity for reflection and for acting differently, even if that capacity is reduced by the habit. The vicious person who lies or steals or bullies may still be capable of recognizing what they are doing and choosing otherwise — and their failure to do so is their responsibility.

But Aristotle also recognizes limits: if the malformation is severe enough, genuine virtue may not be achievable. This is not a counsel of despair but a recognition that moral formation is a communal, not just individual, responsibility. It is the business of the city — the community — to create conditions in which people can be well formed. The political community has an ethical obligation to be a good community.

💪 WHAT YOU HAVE LEARNED

Virtue is a stable disposition of character acquired through repeated practice. The virtuous person does not merely act rightly but feels rightly — takes genuine pleasure in virtuous action. Character can be formed toward virtue or toward vice, and we bear responsibility for our characters because we were responsible for the habits that formed them.
