Flourishing and the Highest Good: Aristotle's Starting Point

📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
What is the highest good for a human being?
Aristotle (384–322 BC) was Plato's most gifted student and arguably the most comprehensive philosopher in the Western tradition. His Nicomachean Ethics — named for his son Nicomachus and likely compiled from lecture notes — is the foundational text of virtue ethics and remains one of the most carefully argued works in the history of moral philosophy. It begins with a question that neither Bentham nor Kant thought to ask first: what is the highest good for a human being? And it gives an answer: eudaimonia, which is most accurately translated as flourishing rather than happiness, because what Aristotle means is not a feeling but a way of living well and doing well.

1
I will be able to explain why Aristotle begins ethics with the question of the highest good rather than with the question of right action.
2
I will be able to define eudaimonia accurately and explain why "happiness" is a misleading translation.
3
I will be able to evaluate Aristotle's argument that eudaimonia is the highest good, and explain why pleasure, honor, and wealth fail as candidates for this role.
📚 PART 1 · THE OPENING QUESTION
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
When you think about living well, what comes to mind? What would your life have to be like for you to genuinely say it is going well?

Every Action Aims at Some Good

Aristotle opens the Nicomachean Ethics with a sentence that seems obvious but has enormous consequences: "Every art and every inquiry, and similarly every action and pursuit, is thought to aim at some good; and for this reason the good has rightly been declared to be that at which all things aim" (NE I.1). If every action aims at something, then there must be some final good at which all actions ultimately aim — a good that is desired for its own sake, not as a means to something else. Aristotle calls this the highest good.

What is this highest good? All people agree on the name: eudaimonia. But they disagree substantially about what eudaimonia actually consists in. Some say it is pleasure; some say it is honor; some say it is wealth; some say it is virtue. Aristotle's task in Book I is to work out which of these, if any, is right.

📚 PART 2 · WHAT EUDAIMONIA IS NOT

Why "Happiness" Is a Misleading Translation

THE KEY TERM
eudaimonia
Greek roots:
eu (well) + daimon (spirit or divine self)
Literally: to have a good spirit; to be well-spirited
Best translation:
Flourishing, living well, doing well
Not: happiness (a feeling) or pleasure (a sensation)

"Happiness" translates eudaimonia in most English editions, but it is misleading because "happiness" in ordinary English refers to a pleasant feeling, a subjective state of mind. Someone can be happy in this sense while their life is going quite badly — through ignorance of their circumstances, self-deception, or artificially induced contentment. Aristotle's eudaimonia is not a feeling; it is an activity. It is something you do and are, not something that happens to you or that you simply feel. A person cannot be flourishing in Aristotle's sense while actually living badly, any more than a plant can be flourishing while actually withering.

Three Failed Candidates

Aristotle considers and rejects three candidates for the highest good in Book I, Chapter 5:

Pleasure is pursued by the "life of enjoyment" as the highest good. Aristotle does not dismiss pleasure: it is a genuine good that accompanies excellent activity. But pleasure alone cannot be the highest good, because it is something we share with animals, and because a life devoted purely to pleasure — what Aristotle calls the "life of gratification" — is the life of a slave or beast, not of a person exercising distinctively human capacities. Bentham's hedonic calculus is, on Aristotle's analysis, the philosophical expression of this mistaken view.

Honor is what the "political life" pursues as its highest good. But honor depends on those who bestow it, not on the person who receives it. Someone could be honored for reasons that have nothing to do with their actual excellence. Honor is something that happens to us, not something we do. Therefore it cannot be the highest good, which must belong to a person as her own.

Wealth is not even a candidate for the highest good in Aristotle's analysis: it is purely instrumental, sought as a means to other things. No one desires wealth for its own sake; they desire it for what it enables them to do and have. The person who treats wealth as an end in itself has confused a means with the good itself.

📚 PART 3 · THE MARKS OF THE HIGHEST GOOD

What Any Candidate for the Highest Good Must Be

Before identifying eudaimonia, Aristotle establishes what any candidate for the highest good must satisfy. There are three formal requirements:

Requirement What it means Why pleasure fails it
Self-sufficient Adding it to a life makes life more desirable by itself, not by adding another kind of good We always want to add more to a merely pleasant life; it does not by itself make life complete
Final Desired for its own sake, never merely as a means to something else We often desire pleasure as a sign of health or excellence, not merely for itself
Complete Nothing further needs to be added; the good life with it is lacking in nothing A life of pleasure that also contains no friendship, no achievement, no moral excellence seems to be missing something important

Eudaimonia satisfies all three. It is desired for its own sake, not as a means to something else. Adding it to a life makes the life more desirable in a way that adding wealth or pleasure cannot. And the flourishing life is complete: nothing is lacking that a human being genuinely needs to live well. The next lesson examines how Aristotle grounds this in his account of human nature and the concept of telos.

💪 WHAT YOU HAVE LEARNED

Aristotle begins where neither Bentham nor Kant began: with the question of what living well actually means for beings like us. His answer — eudaimonia, flourishing — is not a feeling but an activity, not something that happens to us but something we do and are. Neither pleasure, nor honor, nor wealth satisfies the three formal marks of the highest good. The next lesson examines what grounds eudaimonia in human nature itself: Aristotle's function argument and the concept of telos.
