📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
What does Aristotle's ethics get right that consequentialism and deontology miss, and what does it still leave open?
This lesson brings together the nine weeks of Quarter 3. You have worked through Aristotle's account of eudaimonia, the function argument, practical wisdom, habituation and character formation, the doctrine of the mean, the cardinal virtues, friendship, and weakness of will. The goal today is not to review those topics but to understand the architecture that connects them, to see clearly what virtue ethics contributes that the theories of Quarters 1 and 2 could not supply, and to identify the question that virtue ethics raises but does not fully answer: a question that will drive Quarter 4.

📚 PART 1 · THE INTERNAL LOGIC OF VIRTUE ETHICS
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
Before reading on: in your own words, what is the single most important idea you have taken from this quarter's study of Aristotle?

The Chain of Argument

Aristotle's ethics is not a collection of independent theses but a single connected argument. Here is its structure:

THE LOGICAL STRUCTURE OF ARISTOTLE'S ETHICS
1
Eudaimonia as the highest good. Every action aims at some good; the highest good must be final, self-sufficient, and complete. It is eudaimonia: flourishing or living and doing well.
2
The function argument grounds virtue. Humans have a characteristic function: active exercise of the soul in accordance with reason. Excellence at this function is virtue. Therefore, eudaimonia consists in virtuous activity.
3
Character is formed by habituation. Virtues are stable dispositions of desire and action toward the mean. They are acquired by practice: you become courageous by doing courageous things. Character is not fate but achievement.
4
Phronesis governs the virtues. Practical wisdom perceives what the particular situation calls for. It is the master virtue that prevents the moral virtues from misfiring. Without it, courage becomes recklessness, generosity becomes waste.
5
Friendship and community complete the account. Virtue-friendship is not an ornament but a necessity: it enables virtuous activity, self-knowledge, and the continued formation of character. Human beings are social animals and cannot flourish in isolation.
📚 PART 2 · WHAT VIRTUE ETHICS CONTRIBUTES

Three Things Virtue Ethics Gets Right

Question Consequentialism Deontology Virtue Ethics
What is the fundamental question of ethics? Which action produces the best outcomes? Which action conforms to the moral law? What kind of person should I be, and how should I live?
What is the role of desire and emotion? Relevant only as inputs to welfare calculations. Largely irrelevant: the moral worth of an act depends on the will, not the feeling. Central: the virtuous person desires the right things. Virtue requires integration of desire and reason.
What is the role of moral formation? Negligible: anyone who calculates correctly can act rightly. Minimal: anyone who applies the categorical imperative can act rightly. Essential: virtue cannot be achieved by instruction alone; it requires habituation over time.
What is the unit of moral assessment? Individual acts or policies. Individual acts conforming to universal law. Whole lives and persons.
What grounds the theory? The value of welfare or utility. The demands of practical reason. The nature of human beings and their characteristic good.
📚 PART 3 · THE OPEN QUESTION AND THE TURN TO NATURAL LAW

What Virtue Ethics Leaves Open

Virtue ethics is the most complete and psychologically realistic of the three ethical theories you have studied. But it raises a deep question it does not fully answer. Aristotle grounds virtuous activity in human nature: the virtues are what enable human beings to fulfill their characteristic function. But this raises two connected questions:

  1. Why does human nature set the standard? If human beings have a nature and that nature grounds the virtues, what grounds human nature? On what basis do we say that the function of a human being is rational activity rather than, say, the pursuit of pleasure or the accumulation of power?
  2. What is the source of moral obligation? Aristotle tells us that virtuous activity is excellent activity and that it constitutes the good life. But he does not fully explain why excellent activity is obligatory: why you must pursue it rather than merely being better off if you do.

Natural law theory, developed most fully by Thomas Aquinas in the 13th century (drawing directly on Aristotle), attempts to answer both questions. It argues that human nature is itself ordered: that there is a natural end or telos built into what it means to be a human being, and that this order is grounded in the structure of reason and, for Aquinas, in the creative act of a rational God. Moral obligation, on this account, is not an arbitrary imposition but a reflection of what human beings actually are and what they are for.

THE PHILOSOPHICAL BRIDGE: FROM VIRTUE ETHICS TO NATURAL LAW
Aristotle (384–322 BC)
Virtue is excellence at the human function. The good life is virtuous activity. Human nature is the standard.
→
Open Question
What grounds human nature itself? Why does reason set the standard for human excellence? What is the source of moral obligation?
→
Aquinas (1225–1274)
Human nature has a telos grounded in reason and in the order of creation. Natural law is reason's participation in the divine order that governs all things.

💪 WHAT YOU HAVE LEARNED

Virtue ethics asks a different and more fundamental question than consequentialism or deontology: not "What should I do?" but "What kind of person should I be?" Aristotle's answer is a complete and internally coherent account of human flourishing, character formation, practical wisdom, the virtues, friendship, and moral failure. What it does not fully provide is a grounding for human nature itself: an answer to the question of what makes human nature the standard for human excellence, and what makes virtuous activity genuinely obligatory. That is the question with which Quarter 4 begins.
